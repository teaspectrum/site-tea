Georgian tea is the original Soviet tea story: Liu Junzhou established commercial gardens at Chakvi in 1893, the Soviet system turned them into a giant industry, the 1990s collapse followed, and small estates are now rebuilding along the Black Sea.

The short answer

Georgia was the Soviet Union's most important tea region, concentrated in Adjara, Guria, Samegrelo and Abkhazia on the eastern Black Sea. Liu Junzhou established commercial tea at Chakvi in 1893. The Soviet system expanded plantations to about 67,000 ha and harvested 152,000 t of leaf in 1985, supplying roughly 95% of Soviet tea. The industry collapsed after 1991; small orthodox estates have revived since the 2000s. This is a Black Sea origin with its own cultivars and processing history, not an appendage of Russian tea.

Geography and growing conditions

The eastern Black Sea coast is protected by the sea and the Caucasus, with mild winters, high humidity and abundant rain. Gardens sit mainly on hilly ground at 0–500 m, on acidic soils. Batumi and Chakvi in Adjara are the core; Guria, Samegrelo and Abkhazia also grow tea. The season runs 120–150 days, with both machine and hand picking. Maritime air keeps frost risk low; summer downpours and drainage are the main management issues.

History and industry

The first trials date to 1845; Goraberezhouli in Guria followed in 1847. In 1893 Konstantin Popov brought Liu Junzhou, Chinese workers, seed and equipment to Chakvi; the tea won a gold medal at the 1900 Paris exposition. By 1921 plantations covered 915.5 ha. Soviet collectivisation built the Anaseuli Institute of Tea and Subtropical Crops and bred Kolkhida, Gruziya and other cultivars. Output peaked in 1985, collapsed with the Soviet Union, and small factories returned in the 2000s.

Bush, cultivars and processing

Kolkhida/Kolkhuri, Gruziya and Kolkheti are the best-known local selections: cold-hardy, high-yielding and suited to mechanical harvest. Soviet processing favoured CTC, broken leaf and pressed bricks, with volume as the goal. Today small estates make orthodox black, green and oolong by hand: withering, rolling, oxidation and drying. Old factories and new equipment coexist, with small batches chasing aroma rather than weight.

Tea types and liquor

The Soviet era is associated with strong, bitter CTC black tea and bricks, usually sweetened or served with jam. Today's orthodox black has a bright red liquor with plum, malt and floral notes; green tea is grassy and nutty; oolong is fruitier. Georgians still drink tea strong and sweet, with the samovar and glass tumbler remaining part of daily life. Specialty tea is growing in Tbilisi and Batumi.

Industry and market

In 1985 the system covered 67,000 ha, harvested 152,000 t of leaf and employed about 180,000 people. Today's area and output are far below that peak, and many old gardens are abandoned. Private estates, cooperatives and exporters make specialty tea for Russia, Turkey and the EU. Brands are weak and prices low; tourism and estate visits are new income.

Brewing and visiting

Orthodox black: 90–95°C, 3 minutes. Green: 80–85°C. Oolong: 90°C, short infusions. May–October is the easiest time in Adjara; Chakvi is about 9 km from Batumi. Guria's gardens and old factories are more scattered and need a local guide. Abkhazia has separate travel restrictions and needs its own assessment.

Misconceptions and limits

Georgian tea is just bad Soviet tea is too simple. The Soviet system pushed volume, but it also built cultivars, agronomy and processing skills; today's revival did not appear from nowhere. Abkhazian gardens are shaped by political and property disputes and should not be conflated with Georgia proper. The 1985 peak is not current output.

From Soviet giant to small estates

After the Soviet collapse, Georgian tea suffered three blows at once: collective farms dissolved, the Russian market disappeared, and old factories became obsolete. Through the 1990s many gardens were cut down or abandoned, and growers turned to hazelnuts, citrus and maize. From the 2000s, private estates, cooperatives and foreign investors began restoring some old gardens: some replanted Kolkhida, others brought in new clones, and processing shifted from CTC toward orthodox leaf, hand picking and aroma. Output is still far below the 1985 peak, but a quality narrative and tourism income have made tea visible again. Abkhazian gardens remain outside the mainstream supply chain because of political and property disputes; Guria and Samegrelo have older, more scattered factories and recover more slowly than the Chakvi/Batumi area of Adjara. Replanting old gardens is expensive and nursery stock is short — the main bottleneck for revival.

The 152,000 t figure for 1985 is green leaf, not made tea. At the roughly 4:1 ratio of the time, finished tea was about 30,000–40,000 t — a figure that cannot be compared directly with today’s specialty output. Clarifying the unit prevents the Soviet peak from being mistaken for current capacity.
