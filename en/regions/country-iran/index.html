Iran's tea belt lies on the Caspian's southern shore in Gilan and Mazandaran: Lahijan gardens, double-pot brewing and sugar lumps make the everyday chai.

The short answer

Iranian tea concentrates in Gilan and Mazandaran on the Caspian coast, with Lahijan the most famous tea town. Iran began scaled planting around 1900 and became a significant producer in West Asia by the mid-twentieth century. Today black tea dominates, consumption is huge, and imports are blended with local leaf. Read Iran by connecting the Caspian garden belt to nationwide teahouse culture.

Geography and growing conditions

Gilan and Mazandaran sit between the northern slope of the Alborz and the Caspian, humid and rainy — a sharp contrast to Iran's arid interior. Gardens mostly lie at 0–500 metres on acid soils, hot and humid in summer, mild in winter. Caspian cloud and rain are essential; dry years require irrigation.

Terroir and craft

Iranian tea is concentrated on the Caspian coast in Gilan and Mazandaran, with Lahijan as the core, from sea level to about 1,000 metres. Chinese-derived cultivars are made mainly into orthodox black tea, with a little green. Caspian humidity and mountain cloud slow growth, and plucking runs from spring into autumn.

History and industry

Iran introduced tea from India and China around 1900, with the first garden at Lahijan; the Pahlavi period expanded planting, and output reached a relatively high level in the 1970s. After the 1979 revolution, gardens were nationalised and later partly privatised; war and sanctions affected equipment and exports. Today a state tea organisation regulates the sector, and local leaf is blended with imports for the domestic market.

Tea types and everyday drinking

Black tea dominates. The traditional method uses a double pot on a samovar: a strong concentrate diluted with hot water, served in a small glass with a sugar lump held in the mouth. Green tea exists but is less common; herbal, rose and saffron teas are also popular. Iranian black tea is strong and dark, with sweetness at the centre; local leaf often shows smoky, woody and sweet notes.

Industry and market

The sector is managed through state and cooperative structures, with Lahijan the main processing and brand centre. Domestic output covers part of demand and imports fill the rest. Drinking is strong black tea with sugar cubes, often beside a samovar; single-garden specialty is rare and prices follow exchange rates and import policy.

The cup and brewing

Iranian black tea is deep red and strong, with malt, wood and a light smoky note, usually sweetened with sugar or rock sugar. Plain, it can turn astringent, so avoid a long steep. Brew at 95°C for three to four minutes; a samovar keeps intensifying the liquor. Visit Lahijan from April to October, allowing for Caspian rain.

Buying and boundaries

Local output is below demand, and the market blends imports heavily; check the local-leaf share and origin. Single-garden Lahijan or Gilan tea is rare; a named garden and year is a better signal. Brew at 95–100°C, double-pot strong and diluted; sugar is traditional, so plain-tea standards do not apply.

Myths and boundaries

“Iran only drinks imported tea” is inaccurate. Iran has a century-old industry and the Lahijan origin, but output is insufficient and imports supplement it. Nor is it part of the mint-tea world: Iranians more often drink plain black tea with sugar.

Lahijan and Gilan

Iranian tea is almost entirely on the Caspian coast of Gilan and Mazandaran, around Lahijan, Fuman and Rudsar: low altitude, heavy rain and fog. Plants came from India in 1899, and state gardens were built in the 1920s–30s. Today smallholders dominate, factories are old, and the crop is mainly black tea; domestic demand is huge and imports fill the gap. The Caspian gardens are wet in spring and hot in summer, picking runs from April to October, and the strong liquor is drunk with sugar and a samovar.

Source boundary

The tea area is only on the Caspian coast, not nationwide; local output and consumption are far apart, so blending and imports are normal. The institutional changes before and after 1979 are large; historical output is not today's scale.
