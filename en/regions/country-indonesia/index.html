Indonesia

Gambung Green Tea (Gamboeng Peko Super)

Gambung Green Tea, in the public record, is the PPTK product **Gamboeng Green Tea Peko Super** — not a generic “Gambung village” tea and not a cultivar. PPTK's official page defines it as a Grade I green tea made from young Assamica and Sinensis shoots picked to the p+1 and b+1m standards. The leaf is round-rolled and glossy green, the taste is refreshingly astringent, the aroma has a natural roasted note, the liquor is yellow-green, and the laboratory total polyphenol figure is 21–21.5%. The official brewing suggestion is 2–3 g per 150–200 ml at 70–80°C for 2–3 minutes. Gambung itself is a roughly 600-hectare estate in Ciwidey, Bandung Regency, most of it tea and the rest natural forest.

Indonesia

Kerinci Tea (Kayu Aro Black Tea)

Kerinci tea usually means black tea from the Kerinci district of Jambi, Sumatra, and its core is the Kayu Aro garden and factory. The main garden sits at about 1,600 m on the western slope of Mount Kerinci; the factory was built in 1925 by the Dutch company NV HVA, began formal production in the early 1930s, and is now managed by PTPN IV PalmCo. It makes both orthodox and CTC black tea. The old draft called it Indonesia’s highest and largest tea region and described snow on a 3,805 m summit. Mount Kerinci is 3,805 m, but it is equatorial and has no permanent snow; the tea garden is nowhere near the summit.

Indonesia

Malabar Tea Estate (Pangalengan, West Java)

"Malabar Mountain Tea" refers to the Malabar Tea Estate in Pangalengan, West Java, not a separate cultivar and not a generic "mountain garden." The estate was opened by Karel Albert Rudolf Bosscha in 1896 and is now managed by PTPN I Regional 2. A 2003 report gives an area of about 1,300 ha; a 2025 official source puts the tea at 1,400–1,600 m, hand-picked by fine plucking (two leaves and a bud), and identifies Leafy Grade Premium OP FO as the representative black tea. Its flavour is floral with a strong body, and its first export to Taiwan in 2025 came with a potential 42 t/year purchase interest. The old draft's century-old bushes, volcanic-stone cellar and geothermal-mineral story had no reliable source and have been removed.

Indonesia

Papandayan White Tea (Garut Highland White Tea)

Papandayan white tea is this site’s regional label for white tea from the Garut highlands of West Java, Indonesia. It is not a registered origin and not a single-estate brand. What can be verified: a Garut highland producer, Dasep Badrusalam of Mooi Tea House / Teh Nyaneut, made white tea and picked buds only between 05:30 and 07:30; and PTPN VIII operates a tea estate called Papandayan in Garut, but the source describes it as a black-tea estate. The old draft merged the two into “the only white-tea micro-estate on Papandayan’s southern slope at 1,600–1,800 m” and invented a founder, cultivar and awards. Those claims have no source and are not retained.

Indonesia is Southeast Asia's largest tea producer: West Java, Sumatra and a few highland gardens supply black, green and jasmine tea to domestic and export markets.

The short answer

Indonesian tea concentrates in West Java — Puncak, Bandung, Sukabumi — and Sumatra around Medan and Simalungun. Many Java gardens date to the Dutch colonial period. Black tea is the bulk, with green, jasmine and a little oolong; domestic drinking leans sweet and iced. Read Indonesia through three layers: colonial estate legacy, smallholders and factories, and domestic brands.

Geography and growing conditions

Java's volcanic belt has fertile soils; gardens mostly sit at 800–1,500 metres, cooler and wet. North Sumatra's highlands are lower and more humid. On the equator, tea can be picked year-round, but the wet season shapes plucking and withering. Volcanic ash, steep slopes and erosion are long-term issues.

Terroir and craft

Indonesian tea is concentrated in West Java around Puncak, Bandung and Sukabumi, and in Sumatra around Medan and Kerinci, at roughly 800–1,500 metres. Assam and local clones were planted under Dutch rule, and CTC coexists with orthodox. Heat and humidity push leaf fast; fermentation control and factory equipment determine cleanliness.

History and industry

The modern industry is a three-layer build. In 1684 the German botanist Andreas Cleyer brought tea seed from Japan to Batavia for ornamental and trial plots. In 1828 Governor-General Van den Bosch folded tea into the Cultivation System, and commercial gardens appeared in Java. In 1877 Rudolf Kerkhoven brought assamica seed from Ceylon; it gradually replaced China-type bushes and became the base of today’s strong CTC tea. By the early 20th century the Dutch East Indies was one of the largest tea exporters outside India and Ceylon. The Japanese occupation of 1942–45 converted gardens to food crops and output collapsed. After independence, Dutch estates were nationalised, and by 1965 exports were only 53% of pre-war volume. Prices have long been set by auctions and export contracts, leaving smallholders with weak bargaining power; domestic jasmine-tea blending supports part of the green-tea capacity. Today output is about 120,000 t, eighth in the world in 2023, mostly from West Java; smallholders hold the largest area, CTC dominates, and green tea supplies local jasmine blends.

Tea types and everyday drinking

Black tea dominates, both CTC and orthodox, deep and strong for tea bags and milk tea; green is mostly domestic; jasmine is a major category; small amounts of oolong and white appear in the highlands. Everyday drinking is sweet: iced tea, sweetened black tea, jasmine. Single-garden premium tea is weak.

Industry and market

Indonesia is a significant producer. CTC serves domestic tea bags and milk tea, while orthodox enters international blends and specialty. Auction and export systems are established, but single-garden specialty is a small share. Sweet black tea dominates domestic drinking, and estate tourism is growing in West Java and Sumatra.

The cup and brewing

Indonesian CTC is deep red and strong, with malt and wood, well suited to milk. Orthodox is cleaner, with flower, fruit and honey. Brew CTC at 95–100°C with milk; orthodox at 90–95°C and plain. West Java visits are best from May to September; Sumatra roads slow in the rains.

Buying and boundaries

For Indonesian black tea, check origin (Java or Sumatra), CTC versus orthodox and year. Sumatra is often stronger and earthier; Java highlands are brighter. For jasmine, check natural flower aroma and fresh base. Brew CTC at 95–100°C with milk and sugar; green at 85°C. Watch for mould in tropical storage.

Myths and boundaries

“Indonesia only has coffee” ignores a century-old tea industry. Indonesia was a major Dutch colonial tea producer, and West Java and Sumatra still have commercial gardens. International brand visibility is low and CTC dominates volume.

Source boundary

Indonesia is a major producer but less internationally visible than India or Sri Lanka; its strength is commodity black tea and jasmine. Java and Sumatra differ, so “Indonesian tea” is not one flavour. Colonial estate history and today's smallholder structure should be read separately.
