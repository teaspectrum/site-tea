Abigail's Blend

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.

Hawaii Volcano Green Tea

Hawaii volcano green tea is a regional name for green tea grown on the volcanic highlands of Hawaiʻi Island, especially around Volcano at about 4,000 ft and the north-east slope of Mauna Loa at about 3,000 ft. It is not a single brand, and not all Hawaiian tea comes from volcanic highlands. Tea was introduced to Hawaiʻi in 1887. In 1999–2001, USDA/ARS and the University of Hawaiʻi CTAHR established clonal trials at Waiakea (600 ft), Mealani (2,800 ft) and Volcano (4,000 ft); Mealani and Volcano produced excellent green and oolong tea. Big Island Tea, founded in 2001, makes small-batch handmade green and black tea on a volcanic-ash garden at about 3,000 ft on Mauna Loa. Volcano Winery, at about 4,000 ft near the Kilauea summit, took cuttings in 2005, planted in 2006, reached one acre by 2011 and makes black and white tea. Tea Hawaii works in native-forest agroforestry at Volcano Village. Public sources do not give a single “volcanic mineral” sensory standard, so volcanic-ash soil cannot be treated as a guaranteed flavour.

Southern Sweet Tea

Sweet tea is a southern United States way of drinking iced tea, especially associated with South Carolina and Georgia; it is not a tea category, cultivar or brand. The usual method is to brew black tea strong and hot, dissolve a large amount of sugar while the tea is still hot, cool it, and pour it over ice, often with lemon. An early published “Iced Tea” recipe appears in the 1878 edition of Housekeeping in Old Virginia (often cited as 1879): one quart of boiling water and two teaspoons of green tea, strained and poured over ice with two teaspoons of granulated sugar per glass, with lemon optional. That is “iced tea with sugar”, not the modern concentrate sweetened heavily before cooling. South Carolina designated sweet tea its official “state hospitality beverage” in 1995. Summerville calls itself the “Birthplace of Sweet Tea”, but the official tourism page hedges with “history seems to indicate such”; in 2016 the town used 2,524 gallons of sweet tea to set a Guinness record. The origin of sweet tea is not established, and local legend should not be presented as proven history.

The USA is not a traditional tea origin, but a laboratory of modern tea consumption: iced tea, tea bags, bottled tea, specialty tea and coffee culture collide here. Charleston Tea Garden and Hawaii are the few commercial gardens; most “American tea” is brand, blend and setting.

The short answer

The USA has no large traditional tea region. Commercial leaf comes mainly from Charleston Tea Garden in South Carolina, Hawaii and a few trial gardens; almost all tea is imported. Iced tea, tea bags, bottled tea, milk tea and specialty tea define consumption, with coffee as the main competitor. Read the USA by separating domestic growing from imported brands, then southern iced tea, specialty tea and coffee culture.

Geography and growing conditions

The USA spans many climates, but commercial tea growing is limited: Charleston in South Carolina has a historic garden and factory, Hawaii has volcanic-soil gardens, and California, Oregon and Washington have trials. Southern heat and humidity, Hawaiian volcanic soil and altitude, and California’s Mediterranean climate all have potential, but labour, land cost and processing scale limit commercial volume.

Consumption geography is clearer: southern iced tea is deep, the Northeast and West Coast have strong specialty tea, tea rooms and Asian tea communities, and the Midwest leans on coffee and tea bags. US geography explains settings more than a tea-origin map.

History and industry

Tea arrived with British colonists in the seventeenth century; the Boston Tea Party of 1773 became a symbol of independence. Nineteenth-century US merchants traded directly with China and Japan, building import and blending systems. Iced tea spread after the 1904 St. Louis World’s Fair, and tea bags, bottled tea and fast food shaped the twentieth century.

Domestic commercial gardens were tried in the late nineteenth century; Charleston Tea Garden is a rare survivor, and Hawaii developed in the twenty-first. Today almost all leaf is imported, with brands, specialty shops, milk tea and bottled tea forming the market. Coffee is strong; tea serves health, convenience and niche settings.

Tea types and flavour

US tea is led by black tea bags and iced tea, with green, herbal, oolong, white and puer growing in specialty. Southern iced tea is sweet with lemon, cool and refreshing; milk tea and matcha are popular with younger buyers. Specialty tea emphasises single origin, brewing and tea-room experience.

US iced tea is usually strong-brewed, chilled and sweetened with lemon; tea bags take boiling water for three to five minutes; specialty green at 80°C, oolong at 90–95°C. The range is wide; “American tea” is not one flavour.

Cultivars and craft

Domestic gardens use Chinese and Assam introductions; Charleston makes black and green tea from Camellia sinensis, Hawaii makes green, oolong and black. Processing is small, partly hand and partly machine; imported tea relies on source-country craft and blending.

The other half of US craft is blending, bagging, bottling and cold brewing: brands design by flavour, price and convenience, with iced and bottled tea prioritising stability and sweetness. Check source, blend and sugar, not just the US brand.

Market and grades

The market is led by tea bags, iced tea, bottled tea and milk tea, with specialty tea, tea rooms and single origins growing. Prices range from supermarket bags to specialty gifts. Coffee chains and energy drinks compete; tea serves health, caffeine-free and afternoon settings. The USA is also an import and brand-incubation market.

Buy fresh bags and iced tea for daily use; single-origin and clear craft for specialty; green by freshness, oolong by roast, black by strength. Brand and source matter more than the “USA” label.

The cup and brewing

Iced tea: strong-brew black tea at 95°C for five minutes, sweeten with lemon, chill; cold brew is softer. Tea bags at 95°C for three to five minutes; green at 80°C; oolong at 90–95°C. Southern sweet tea is high in sugar; specialty tea rooms lean plain. Visit Charleston for the garden and factory, book Hawaii gardens, and find specialty tea rooms in New York and San Francisco.

Myths and boundaries

“The USA grows no tea” is inaccurate: Charleston and Hawaii have commercial gardens, but volume is tiny. “American tea equals iced tea” ignores specialty and bags. “Iced tea is low-end” ignores cold brew and specialty iced tea. “American tea equals British tea” ignores immigrant and Asian tea communities.

The US tea area is not a national region; administrative borders differ from gardens, and brand or source country cannot replace batch. This page gives structure; tea pages and brand information carry specifics.
