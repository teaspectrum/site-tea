Tea in Papua New Guinea

Papua New Guinea

Almost all of the country's commercial tea comes from one highland basin: the Wahgi Valley.

The short answer

PNG's commercial tea is not a coastal or island-wide crop. It is concentrated in the Wahgi Valley of the central highlands, around Mount Hagen and Banz, at roughly 1,200–1,950 m. The gardens are planted mainly with seedling assamica stock and processed overwhelmingly as CTC black tea. This is a late-colonial industrial tea region, kept alive after independence by a small number of estates — not a romantic Pacific-island tea story.

Geography and growing conditions

The Wahgi Valley is a broad, fertile highland basin with about 2,500 mm of annual rain, deep soils and good drainage. Altitude cools the growing season, so flushing is slower than in the lowlands. The valley floor was originally swampy grassland; commercial gardens were drained and planted from the 1960s. Steep surrounding slopes, concentrated rainfall and difficult road access limit expansion, while the hot, humid lowlands are unsuitable for commercial Camellia.

History

Commercial tea was first envisaged in 1939, when the Highlands Agricultural Experiment Station was established at Aiyura. A small quantity of seed came from Sarawak; 88 seedlings were raised, and 13 bushes were selected as seed bearers. Four acres of plucking tea were planted at about 6,000 ft between 1947 and 1949. Expansion began in the 1960s, and the first tea factory opened at Warrawau near Mount Hagen in 1965. Estates expanded to roughly 1,800 ha, with Carpenter Estates the dominant group. After independence in 1975, land, labour and market arrangements changed, but the Wahgi Valley remained the centre.

Bush, cultivars and processing

The gardens are largely Camellia sinensis var. assamica grown from seed: broad-leaved, vigorous and well suited to the wet highlands and CTC processing. Plucked leaf is withered, cut-tear-curled, fermented and dried into a strong, fast-infusing broken black tea. A small amount of orthodox tea is made for local specialty buyers. Factories sit close to the gardens, so leaf travels short distances — a real quality advantage in a wet climate.

Tea types and liquor

CTC black tea dominates: deep red liquor, high strength, malt and woody notes, built for milk and sugar. Green and oolong lots are experimental or micro-scale. Everyday consumption is dominated by imported tea bags; local tea goes into local brands and a small export trade. Highland is a growing condition, not an automatic guarantee of specialty quality.

Industry and market

Volumes are small and international visibility is low. Production is concentrated in one valley, brands and certification are weak, and infrastructure and land tenure are persistent constraints. Recent organic and specialty narratives exist, but they do not add up to a national industry. Coffee tourism and estate visits bring attention; tea itself still behaves like a commodity.

Brewing and visiting

CTC: 95–100°C, 3–4 minutes, with milk. Orthodox black: 90–95°C, about 3 minutes. The highlands are easiest in the May–September dry season; roads around Mount Hagen and Banz and security need planning. Factory visits are not a standard tourist product and usually require an estate or industry contact.

Misconceptions and limits

PNG has no tea is wrong. PNG is covered in tea gardens is also wrong. Commercial tea is essentially one basin, and it is mostly CTC. It is not an ancient wild-tea or old-tree region.

The estate economy after independence

After 1975 the Wahgi Valley gardens were not broken up as thoroughly as some African estates. Land claims are layered across families, provincial government and customary arrangements, and estates survive through leases and community agreements; groups such as Carpenter Estates still run several gardens and factories. The highlands are densely populated, so land disputes, labour costs and road maintenance are constant issues. Since the 2000s, volatile coffee prices have brought some growers back to tea, but tea demands more technical and market know-how. Today's highland tea behaves like a stable commodity crop, not a fast-growing star. In the factory, CTC lines cut leaf into uniform particles and fermentation time sets colour and strength; wet-season leaf needs longer withering. Lots are graded by leaf and liquor, mostly for bulk and tea-bag supply, with a small orthodox line for local specialty and export samples.

Source boundary

Commercial tea is essentially confined to the Wahgi Valley, not nationwide. It was built in the late colonial period and kept alive by a few estates after independence; it is not a Pacific-wide tea power. CTC dominates, and small orthodox or green lots do not represent the country.
