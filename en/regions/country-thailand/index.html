Thailand

Cha DoiChang Oolong Green Tea

Cha DoiChang is a small organic tea garden and tea company in Pong Phrae, Mae Lao district, Chiang Rai, Thailand. It is not Doi Chaang Coffee, the well-known coffee brand from Doi Chang village. The tea garden started in 1999, covers 50 rai (about 8 hectares) at roughly 600 m, uses a natural stream that flows down from Doi Chang, and receives about 2,500 mm of rain a year. The company was founded in 2001 with three shareholders and combines Taiwanese tea-making technology with local know-how. Its line includes Superior, Premium, Pure Green, Red Oolong, Eternity and Peaceful Rest.

Choui Fong Oolong

Choui Fong Oolong is not a single cultivar or a producing region. It is the oolong line of Choui Fong Tea, a family-owned producer in Chiang Rai, Thailand, built mainly on Jin Xuan and Ruan Zhi cultivars and sold as plain, scented and first-flush products.

Thailand

Doi Ang Khang Green Tea (Royal Project Organic Green Tea)

Doi Ang Khang Green Tea is an organic green-tea product from the Royal Project at Doi Ang Khang in Fang District, Chiang Mai, Thailand. It is not a tea cultivar and not a generic name for Thai green tea. The Royal Project product page confirms only that it is organic, unfermented green tea with a nutty or seaweed-like aroma and a mild taste; it does not publish the cultivar, the kill-green method or fixed brewing parameters. The place matters: the station was founded in 1969 as the first Royal Project agricultural research station, at about 1,400 metres on the Thai-Myanmar border, as an alternative to opium cultivation. The page keeps those confirmed layers and refuses the old draft's unverified tea-garden, tree-age and processing claims.

Thailand

Doi Mae Salong Oolong

Doi Mae Salong oolong is a place-based style from the highlands of Chiang Rai, not a single cultivar or official grade. Growers work mainly with Taiwanese cultivars such as Jin Xuan (TTES No.12) and Ruan Zhi (TTES No.17), and the cup can range from floral and milky to honeyed or roasted. The tea culture grew out of a former KMT settlement that moved from opium to legal highland crops.

Thailand

Doi Pui Herbal Tea

Doi Pui is a Hmong village inside Doi Suthep-Pui National Park near Chiang Mai, not a verified green-tea growing area. The traceable “Cha Doi Pui” product is an herbal blend built on jiaogulan, mulberry, stevia, ginseng, safflower and ginkgo — a botanical infusion, not tea from Camellia sinensis. The old Chinese draft invented a university tea plantation; that claim has been removed.

Thailand

Doi Tung Oolong

Doi Tung Oolong is a regional and trade name for oolong tea from the Doi Tung highlands in Chiang Rai, northern Thailand. It is not a current official tea SKU of the DoiTung social enterprise. On 15 January 1987, the Princess Mother arrived at Doi Tung at the age of 87 and launched the Doi Tung Development Project (DTDP), replacing opium income with alternative crops such as coffee and macadamia; the project says it became self-sufficient in 2000. DoiTung's current corporate site lists Coffee and Macadamia, Handicrafts, Café DoiTung, Tourism and Horticulture, with no separate tea business. Third-party tea traders describe gardens at about 1,200–1,400 m or 1,600 m, mainly planted with Taiwanese cultivars such as Ruan Zhi and Jin Xuan, harvested in May–June. The official project pages do not give a single garden elevation, cultivar ratio or sensory standard, so Doi Tung Oolong should be treated as a regional/trade tea, not an official branded product.

Thai tea sits in the northern mountains: gardens in Chiang Rai, Chiang Mai and Mae Hong Son, a miang chewing-tea tradition, Yunnanese migrants and royal projects — a young tea region.

The short answer

Thai tea concentrates in the north and north-east highlands — Chiang Rai, Chiang Mai, Mae Hong Son and Nan — at 800–1,600 metres, under monsoon influence. Oolong, green and black tea dominate, with a northern miang (fermented chewing tea) tradition. In the late twentieth century, Yunnanese migrants, royal projects and Taiwanese technique drove garden expansion. Read Thailand by separating highland ethnic tea customs from modern estates.

Geography and growing conditions

The northern hills rise 800–1,600 metres, with 1,200–2,000 mm of rain, a clear dry season and strong diurnal range. Doi Mae Salong and Doi Tung in Chiang Rai, Doi Ang Khang and Doi Pui in Chiang Mai are the main areas; acid soils and steep slopes make erosion control important. The drier north-east plateau has less tea.

Terroir and craft

Thai tea is concentrated in the northern and northeastern hills: Chiang Rai, Chiang Mai, Mae Hong Son and Loei. Assam, China and Taiwan introductions are made into green, oolong, black and CTC. The Chinese-descended villages of Doi Mae Salong and the royal Doi Tung project are two important threads.

History and industry

Highland communities long grew tea and made miang, steaming and fermenting leaf for chewing. In the 1960s, remnants of Yunnanese Kuomintang forces settled at Doi Mae Salong and brought Yunnan seed and processing; in the 1970s–80s, royal projects at Doi Ang Khang and Doi Tung promoted substitute crops, and Taiwanese technique introduced oolong. Thai tea modernised, but remains smaller than coffee.

Tea types and everyday drinking

Thai oolong, especially four-season and Jin Xuan at Doi Mae Salong, is aromatic and light-bodied; green and black tea serve local and tourist markets; miang is a northern tradition of steamed, fermented leaf wrapped in small balls and chewed, not brewed tea. Thais also drink sweet milk tea and iced tea, and urban tea chains are strong.

Industry and market

Thailand produces a moderate volume. Northern highland tea serves domestic specialty and tourism, while CTC in the south and centre supplies milk tea and tea bags. Thai tea (cha yen), strong black tea with condensed and evaporated milk, is the national drink. Specialty oolong and green are growing in Chiang Rai and Chiang Mai, with limited export.

The cup and brewing

Northern Thai oolong is golden, with flower, milk and fruit. Green tea is fresher, with bean and grass; CTC is strong and forms the base of Thai milk tea. Brew oolong at 90°C and fast; green at 80–85°C; CTC at 95°C with condensed milk. Visit Chiang Rai from November to February, allowing for wet-season roads.

Buying and boundaries

For Thai tea, check origin, elevation and cultivar; Doi Mae Salong oolong is for plain drinking, Doi Tung and royal-project tea have clear brands. Brew green at 80–85°C, oolong at 90–95°C. Miang is chewed, not brewed — read the type. Tourist packaging is common; estate and year matter more.

Myths and boundaries

“Thailand only has Thai milk tea” ignores northern highland oolong and green tea. Cha yen is the national drink, but Chiang Rai and Chiang Mai specialty gardens make a different style. Nor does “royal project” stand for all Thai tea.

Mae Salong and Doi Tung

Northern Thailand has two modern starting points. After 1961, remnant Kuomintang communities were settled at Doi Mae Salong and brought Yunnan seed and Taiwanese oolong cultivars; assamica and oolong populations still dominate. In the 1980s the Mae Fah Luang Foundation promoted tea at Doi Tung as a replacement for opium, building a factory and a brand. Today the highlands of Chiang Rai and Chiang Mai, at 1,200–1,600 m, make oolong, black and green tea, with mountain communities and royal projects still the main organisers. Thai daily drinking, by contrast, runs on sweet iced tea and milk tea.

Mae Salong and Doi Tung both offer picking, processing and homestay experiences and anchor Chiang Rai’s tea tourism. Domestic output cannot meet Thai demand, so large volumes of black and green tea are still imported.

Chiang Rai’s tea season runs from November to April, with the dry season concentrating aroma.

Source boundary

The tea area is in the northern hills, not nationwide; miang is a chewing tea, not Camellia infusion. Thai tea is young and small; this page does not inflate it into a Southeast Asian tea power.
