Mozambican tea sits in the Gurué highlands of Zambézia: colonial gardens, civil-war destruction and recent recovery make it a forgotten African tea area.

The short answer

Mozambican tea concentrates in the Gurué highlands of Zambézia, at 1,000–1,500 metres, cool and wet. Tea was established in the colonial period and nearly destroyed after independence by civil war; small recovery began after the 2000s. Read Mozambique through colonial garden legacy, war rupture and small-scale revival.

Geography and growing conditions

Gurué lies in the central highlands near the Malawi border, with mountains, red soils and forest, cooler than the coast. Gardens mostly sit at 1,000–1,500 metres, where cloud and rain suit tea. Roads, power and processing equipment were damaged in the war, slowing recovery.

Season and terroir

The Gurué highlands crop mainly from November to April, when rain complicates mountain roads and leaf transport. From May to October conditions are drier and gardens need shade and irrigation. Revived estates are still aligning plucking standards with factory throughput, so batch consistency is limited.

History and industry

Portuguese colonisers built gardens and factories in Gurué in the mid-twentieth century, making Mozambique a regional tea producer. After independence in 1975, civil war and nationalisation destroyed the industry and gardens were abandoned. After the 1990s peace, limited private investment restored some planting, but far below colonial scale.

Cultivars and processing

Mozambique’s tea sits in the highlands around Gurué in Zambézia, roughly 1,000–1,500 metres, where Assam hybrids and local selections are made into CTC and orthodox black tea. Colonial gardens were abandoned during the civil war and are being slowly revived by smallholders and old estates; processing equipment and power remain bottlenecks.

Tea types and everyday drinking

Black tea dominates, both CTC and orthodox, deep and strong; some green tea exists. Domestic consumption is sweetened black tea and tea bags; specialty tea is almost absent. Gurué leaf mostly goes into local and regional blends.

Plucking and grades

CTC is sorted into BP, PF, PD and fannings for tea bags and milk tea. Orthodox is graded by OP, BOP and tip, but volumes are small. Check estate, processing site and year; be wary of tourist packs that say “Gurué” without any production detail.

Industry and market

Volume is small and most tea serves domestic and regional buyers, with a little reaching European specialty channels. Gurué revival projects use organic and community narratives to seek a premium, but logistics, finance and climate risk limit scale. New claims such as “Mozambican white tea” need careful checking of origin and processing.

The cup

Gurué orthodox black tea is bright red with medium body, flower, wood and a little citrus. CTC is stronger and faster, suited to tea bags. Revived batches vary in cleanliness; good ones show clear sweetness and light astringency.

Who it suits

For plain-drinkers curious about a small origin and tolerant of batch variation; everyday milk tea can use CTC. Check the named estate and processing site, and do not let “post-war revival” or “organic” stories replace quality judgment.

Buying and boundaries

Output is small and traceability weak; for Gurué tea, ask for the specific estate, cooperative and processing date. Brew black at 95–100°C with milk if desired; green at 85–90°C. Post-war supply chains are unstable, and price and authenticity vary widely.

Visiting and brewing

The Gurué season runs mainly from November to April, when rain makes mountain roads slow. Allow extra travel time for garden visits. Brew CTC at 95–100°C with milk; orthodox at 90–95°C and plain, looking for floral and woody notes without a long steep.

Common misunderstandings

“Mozambican tea disappeared” is too absolute. The civil war destroyed most old gardens, but a recovering tea sector remains around Gurué. It is small and volatile, and colonial-era volumes should not be projected onto today.

Teas in this region

Source boundary

The tea area is only in the Gurué highlands of Zambézia, not nationwide; colonial output is not comparable to today. War and land tenure have shaped the industry; this page does not inflate Mozambique into an emerging African tea power.
