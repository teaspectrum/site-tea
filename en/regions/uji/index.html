Uji is not just “matcha origin” but a fine shaded-green-tea system south of Kyoto: river mist, shaded gardens, tencha, stone milling and tea ceremony turn green tea into gyokuro, matcha and kabusecha.

The short answer

Uji lies south of Kyoto Prefecture on both banks of the Uji River, with core gardens in Uji city, Joyo, Ujitawara, Wazuka and Minami-Yamashiro. It is known for matcha (tencha) and gyokuro, plus kabusecha, sencha and hojicha. Shading for twenty days or more, steaming, de-veining and stone milling are the matcha core. Uji tea dates to the fourteenth century, with temples and tea ceremony driving refinement; today Uji is Japan’s premium matcha and gyokuro brand region. Read Uji by shading and tea type first, then cultivar, craft and brand.

Geography and growing conditions

Uji sits at the southern edge of the Kyoto basin, where the Uji River and tributaries create mist and humidity with moderate day–night range, suiting shaded gardens; soils drain well and plots are small and sloping. Ujitawara and Wazuka are higher and cooler; Joyo and Minami-Yamashiro warmer. Rain is about 1,500–2,000 mm, with plum rain and typhoons affecting plucking.

Uji gardens are mostly smallholders; shade frames, hand-plucking and stone milling are costly, and labour shortage and ageing are issues. Shading time, frame material and cultivar decide matcha colour, umami and bitterness; altitude and microclimate create garden differences.

History and industry

Tea reached Japan from China in the twelfth century, and Uji became a centre for temple and court tea in the fourteenth and fifteenth. In the sixteenth, Sen no Rikyu and tea ceremony drove matcha refinement, and Uji tea became tribute and brand in the Edo period. After Meiji, mechanisation and export expanded, and twentieth-century matcha desserts and tea tourism grew the market.

Today Uji is Japan’s core matcha and gyokuro brand, with gardens, tea merchants and tea-ceremony venues dense; brand protection and GI discussion continue. Domestic matcha and export grow, but high-grade matcha is limited; smallholder-merchant partnerships and contract farming increase.

Tea types and flavour

Uji is mainly matcha (tencha) and gyokuro, with kabusecha, sencha, hojicha and black tea. Matcha is shaded for twenty days or more; tencha is de-veined and stone-milled, giving fine foam, deep umami and seaweed. Gyokuro is shaded twenty days or more, pale green, umami and low astringency. Kabusecha is shaded seven to ten days, between sencha and gyokuro; sencha is steamed and rolled with bean and seaweed notes.

Uji matcha is divided into thin and thick tea, with different powder and whisking; culinary and ceremonial grades differ widely. Gyokuro suits cool, small water; matcha is whisked at about 80°C; sencha at 70–80°C.

Cultivars and craft

Uji plants Asahi, Gokou, Yabukita, Okumidori and Yamakai; Asahi and Gokou suit matcha and gyokuro, Yabukita yields more. Shading, steaming, de-veining, drying and stone milling are the matcha core; stone mills run cool and slow, and fineness and aroma are critical.

Gyokuro runs shading, hand-plucking, steaming, rolling and drying; sencha steams for 30–60 seconds and is rolled and shaped. Uji tea uses hiire for aroma and storage; finished tea is low in moisture and kept dark and dry.

Market and brands

The market is mainly domestic and export, led by matcha desserts, tea ceremony and gifts; brands such as Marukyu Koyamaen, Ito Kyuemon and Nakamura Tokichi cover price bands. High-grade matcha is expensive and limited; gyokuro is niche. Chinese and Western matcha demand drives export, but grade and quality vary widely.

Buy by matcha/gyokuro/sencha first, then shading, cultivar, origin and brand; matcha by colour, fineness, umami and bitterness, gyokuro by umami and liquor. The Uji brand does not mean every garden is in Uji city; distinguish GI from merchant blends.

The cup and brewing

Matcha: sift, 80°C water, whisk in a W motion to foam; thin tea about 1.5–2 g per 60–80 ml, thick tea 3–4 g per 30–40 ml. Gyokuro at 50–60°C for 90–120 seconds with little water; sencha at 70–80°C for 60–90 seconds. Visit Uji for gardens, factories, tea ceremony and old streets; spring plucking and autumn tea events are best, and tea rooms need booking.

Myths and boundaries

“Uji equals matcha” ignores gyokuro, kabusecha and sencha. “The greener the matcha, the better” ignores shading, cultivar and processing; colour is not the only standard. “Uji matcha all comes from Uji city” ignores brands and blends. “Matcha equals tea ceremony” ignores daily drinking and desserts.

Uji is a region and brand system, not one tea; shading, cultivar, craft and brand together define it. This page gives structure; tea pages and producers carry specifics.
