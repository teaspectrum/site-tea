The Philippines has no large commercial tea estate. Tea is scattered across smallholder plots and trial stations in the northern Luzon highlands, while everyday drinking is dominated by imported tea bags, milk tea and ready-to-drink tea.

The short answer

Philippine tea is small-scale, fragmented and largely experimental. Tea was introduced before 1905 and planted occasionally around Baguio and Benguet; the Cordillera has native tea bushes and micro-processing, but no national estate. Since 2023 government, universities and business groups have pushed Cordillera tea development, with Benguet State University leading feasibility work. Keep potential and current reality separate.

Geography and growing conditions

The Cordillera highlands run at about 1,300–2,000 m, with cool temperatures, frequent mist and acidic soils suited to Camellia sinensis var. sinensis. Sagada, Benguet and Mountain Province are representative highlands; slopes are steep, typhoons and heavy rain are common, and roads are difficult. The hot lowlands suit coffee, coconut and fruit, not tea.

History and industry

Tea arrived before 1905 and was planted sporadically in Baguio and Benguet. Under Spanish and American rule the highlands were built around coffee, citrus and vegetables, not tea. After the Second World War, indigenous tea was processed on a very small scale by local groups. From the 2000s, health drinks and milk-tea chains expanded imported tea bags. In 2023 the FFCCCII, DA, DTI, the Benguet provincial government and BSU jointly promoted Cordillera tea farms.

Bush, cultivars and processing

China-type sinensis material dominates because it suits high elevations; assamica hybrids are also trialled. Smallholders pick by hand and process mostly green tea, black tea and oolong-style leaf. Equipment is simple and batches are small; fixing and rolling are bottlenecks. Cool nights help aroma, but heavy rain makes withering difficult.

Tea types and liquor

Local tea is mostly green and lightly oxidised, with a pale liquor, grass, chestnut and floral notes; black tea is rarer and medium in strength. The market is dominated by imported black tea bags, milk tea, RTD tea and flavoured tea. Local leaf appears at tourist sites, farm gates and a few specialty shops. Sagada is known for coffee, not tea.

Industry and market

There is no large commercial garden, and output is far below consumption; imported bags and milk-tea ingredients dominate. Prices range from expensive micro-lots to cheap imported bags. The Cordillera tea plan emphasises alternative crops, erosion control and tourism, but nurseries, processing and branding remain bottlenecks.

Brewing and visiting

Local green tea: 80–85°C, 2 minutes. Black: 90–95°C, 3 minutes. Oolong: 90°C, short infusions. The November–April dry season is easier for Benguet and Sagada; mountain roads and typhoon season need buffer time. Buy local tea by processing date and farm, not by the word highland alone.

Misconceptions and limits

The Philippines has no tea is wrong, but the Philippines is a tea power is also wrong. There are scattered gardens and real potential, not an established industry. Sagada's famous crop is coffee, not tea. Imported tea bags and milk tea remain the everyday default.

The Cordillera tea plan and its limits

The 2023 Cordillera tea plan treats tea as an alternative crop: gardens on sloping uplands can reduce erosion, green leaf travels better than vegetables, and a long picking season suits smallholders. Benguet State University's feasibility work covers cultivars, nurseries, processing and markets, while the FFCCCII and the Chinese embassy have shown interest in technical and financial support. The constraints are real: too few nurseries, expensive processing equipment, limited farmer training, weak brands, and typhoons and heavy rain that disrupt picking. Policy attention has not yet become stable commercial output. Another often-missed point is that indigenous tea is not the same as a commercial Camellia cultivar: some communities harvest related Theaceae plants or local populations, with unstandardised processing and flavour, and these should not be presented as a defined origin tea. Imported tea bags, milk-tea powder and RTD tea remain the everyday base.

Philippine tea demand is growing through milk tea and RTD tea, not household plain brewing; imported broken leaf and tea powder therefore dwarf local leaf tea. As long as the milk-tea chain exists, a few highland gardens will not change the import dependence.
