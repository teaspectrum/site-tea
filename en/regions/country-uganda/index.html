Ugandan tea sits in the western rift and Rwenzori foothills: gardens in Tororo, Kabale and Rwenzori, sending black tea and smallholder leaf into East African and export markets.

The short answer

Ugandan tea spreads across the west and southwest — Tororo, the Jinja area, Kabale and the Rwenzori foothills — at 1,500–2,000 metres. British colonisers introduced tea in the early twentieth century, and it became an important export crop after independence; today CTC black tea dominates, with smallholders supplying leaf. Read Uganda through rift highlands, rains and smallholders.

Geography and growing conditions

Uganda lies on the East African plateau, crossed by the equator, but altitude of 1,000–2,000 metres keeps it mild. The western rift, Rwenzori foothills and Lake Victoria margins are wet. Gardens mostly sit at 1,500–2,000 metres on acid red soils; rains are concentrated, dry seasons need irrigation, and roads and processing are constraints.

Season and terroir

Uganda has two rainy seasons, with plucking peaks usually in March–May and September–November. The cooler Kabale highlands grow leaf more slowly for finer aroma, while Jinja and Tororo are hotter and higher-yielding but coarser. Dry spells and storms both disrupt the plucking calendar.

History and industry

British colonisers introduced tea in the early twentieth century, with gardens in Tororo and the western highlands. After independence, estates were partly nationalised and later privatised. Smallholder planting grew in the late twentieth century, making Uganda an important East African black-tea producer. Domestic consumption and exports coexist.

Cultivars and processing

Tea reached Uganda around 1900 through the Entebbe botanic garden, with commercial planting from the 1920s. Fields now use TRFK clones and Assam hybrids, mostly for CTC black tea with a little orthodox. Two rainy seasons and high western elevations allow year-round cropping, though dry spells and storms disrupt fermentation.

Tea types and everyday drinking

CTC black tea dominates, deep red and strong for tea bags and milk tea; some orthodox and green tea exist. Domestic drinking is sweetened black tea; single-estate specialty is limited. Ugandan leaf often enters East African and European blends.

Plucking and grades

CTC is graded into BP1, PF1, PD and fannings: strong, fast-brewing and suited to milk tea. The small orthodox output is sorted by OP, BOP and tip content. First confirm CTC or orthodox, then estate and season; “African highland” alone says little about the cup.

Industry and market

Tea is an important export crop, with gardens around Tororo, Jinja, Kabale and Rukungiri. Most CTC goes through the Mombasa auction into international blends; domestic demand is mainly sweet milk tea. Smallholders sell leaf outside estate factories, so prices track auction demand closely.

The cup

Ugandan CTC is usually deep red and strong, with malt, cocoa and a hint of smoke: a practical base for milk tea and breakfast blends. Orthodox from the Kabale highlands is finer, with fruit and honey, but scarce. It is not a high-aroma plain-tea origin.

Who it suits

Good for drinkers who like strong tea, milk tea or sweetened black tea. Plain-drinkers can seek small Kabale orthodox lots, accepting the typical East African strength. Those wanting bright floral or lightly oxidised oolong will find little choice.

Buying and boundaries

Most Ugandan tea appears in blends; for single estate, check Tororo, Kabale or Rwenzori, grade and year. Brew CTC at 95–100°C with milk; orthodox at 90–95°C. Smallholder leaf and estate tea differ widely; price is not the only signal.

Visiting and brewing

Visits can run east from Kampala to Jinja and Tororo, or southwest to the Kabale highlands. The two rainy seasons, March to May and September to November, are the best growing periods. Brew CTC at 95–100°C with milk; orthodox at about 90°C and plain, expecting a strong, malty base.

Common misunderstandings

“Ugandan tea is only cheap CTC” misses the range. CTC dominates exports, but highland gardens in Tororo and Kabale have also made orthodox and green tea. Specialty volume is small and rarely reaches export shelves.

Western gardens

Ugandan tea grows in the Toro and Kigezi highlands of the west and around Mbale in the east, at 1,200–1,800 m, mainly as black CTC tea. Smallholders and estates coexist, and most output is exported. Equatorial highlands have a long rainy season, with picking all year and peaks in May–August and October–December.

Source boundary

The tea area is in the west and southwest, not nationwide; CTC commodity tea is the bulk and specialty is small. Colonial history and today's smallholder structure should be read separately.
