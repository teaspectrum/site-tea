Bangladesh is South Asia's second-largest tea producer: Sylhet, Moulvibazar, Habiganj and the Chittagong Hill Tracts connect CTC black tea to auction markets and the world.

The short answer

Bangladesh's tea areas concentrate in the northeast around Sylhet and in the southeast around Chittagong and the hill tracts. Commercial production began at Malnichhara in 1857; the colonial period formed the Surma and Halda valleys; after independence output grew, and after 2002 tea spread to Panchagarh and other northern districts. Read Bangladesh through the system of CTC commodity tea, auctions and estate labour.

Geography and growing conditions

Sylhet sits in the Surma valley, with rolling hills and extreme rainfall — the core of Bangladesh tea. Chittagong, Rangamati and Bandarban are higher and often brighter in the cup. Gardens mostly lie at 50–200 metres, hot and humid, so tea grows year-round and the picking season is long. Monsoon floods, landslides and estate labour conditions are persistent issues.

Terroir and craft

Bangladeshi gardens sit mainly in the Sylhet and Chittagong hills of the northeast and southeast, mostly at 50–300 metres, planted with Assam hybrids and BT clones. CTC dominates, with small orthodox and green output. Hot, humid rains push leaf fast, so frequent plucking and short fermentation are needed for a bright cup.

History and industry

A garden appeared in Chittagong in 1840; Malnichhara in Sylhet was established in 1854 and began commercial production in 1857. British planters brought Assam technology and labour, creating an estate system. After independence in 1971, gardens were largely Bangladeshi-owned and a national tea board regulated the sector. After 2002, Panchagarh, Lalmonirhat and other northern districts began planting, breaking the Sylhet-only pattern.

Tea types and everyday drinking

Black tea dominates; CTC is the bulk, deep red and strong, suited to milk tea and tea bags, with some orthodox, green and spiced tea. Everyday “cha” is black tea boiled with plenty of milk and sugar — strong and sweet. The Chittagong auction is part of the global black-tea price system. Green tea has a small market presence.

Industry and market

Bangladesh is a major South Asian producer, with the Chattogram auction setting much of the trade. CTC supplies domestic milk tea and tea bags, with some export. Estates, smallholders and factories coexist; prices follow auctions, wages and weather. Orthodox specialty is a small share, concentrated on a few gardens.

The cup and brewing

Typical CTC is deep red and strong, with malt and wood: the base of the national milk tea. Orthodox is cleaner, with flower, fruit and honey. Brew CTC at 95–100°C with milk; orthodox at 90–95°C and plain. Garden visits are best from March to November, allowing for wet-season roads.

Buying and boundaries

Check CTC versus orthodox, grade and year. CTC suits milk; orthodox is better plain. Bangladeshi tea is often strong, sweet and full-bodied; do not judge it by Darjeeling aroma standards. Prices are low, but packing and storage vary widely; watch for damp and mould in loose tea. Brew CTC at 95–100°C with milk; orthodox at 90–95°C.

Myths and boundaries

“Bangladesh only makes CTC” fits the volume but ignores small orthodox and green lots in Sylhet. Nor is it a “Darjeeling substitute”: altitude, cultivars and craft differ, and the cup is stronger and more milk-tea oriented.

Sylhet and the Chittagong hills

Bangladeshi tea is concentrated in Sylhet and the Chittagong hill tracts. The British trialled tea in Sylhet in the 1830s, and Malnicherra became the first commercial garden in 1857. After independence in 1971 the estates were nationalised and later largely privatised. Today there are about 160 gardens and output in the 90,000-tonne range, with CTC and orthodox lines side by side and auctions in Chittagong. Smallholder and family gardens have grown, but wet-season flooding and the replanting of old bushes remain difficult.

Sylhet’s gardens are often a century old, and replanting is slow because of ageing bushes and land pressure. Hill gardens in the Chittagong tracts depend more on smallholders and wet-season management; auction prices are chronically low, and export competitiveness is limited by cost and quality.

Source boundary

Bangladesh is a major producer but less visible internationally than India or Sri Lanka; its strength is CTC commodity tea, not high-aroma specialty tea. The northern new areas are still small. Colonial estate history and labour issues should be read separately.
