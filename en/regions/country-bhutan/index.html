Bhutan's most coherent tea garden is not on the southeast border but in Samdrupcholing, Trongsa: a 34-woman cooperative that turned tea once planted around a royal palace into green, black and oolong tea.

The short answer

Bhutan is not a commercial tea power, but it is not tea-less either. The most coherent producer today is the Samdrupcholing cooperative in Trongsa. It revived planting in 2009 and has grown from 47 acres to about 100 acres, with 34 women members from eight villages in Dragteng gewog. It makes green, black and oolong tea plus suja (butter-tea) leaf, and won the Government of India's Best Green Tea Producer award in 2018. Other areas are scattered bushes and trials, not a second commercial district.

Geography and growing conditions

Samdrupcholing sits in the middle hills of Trongsa, near the Black Mountain range, with high elevation, frequent cloud and strong day–night temperature swings. Gardens are terraced into slopes with acidic soils. Cool conditions slow growth and refine aroma, but shorten the picking window and limit labour. Steep terrain and narrow roads keep leaf and processing close together, which suits a small cooperative rather than a large estate.

History and industry

Bhutanese tea is tied to the Second King, Jigme Wangchuck, who brought plants from Sikkim and had them planted around Samdrupcholing Palace for local tea-making. Cultivation was later neglected. In April 2009, 27 founding members formed the Samdrupcholing Ngoja Tshonglay Detshen and restored 47 acres; today the cooperative farms about 100 acres with 34 women members across eight villages. It won the Indian government's Best Green Tea Producer award in 2018, added oolong and black tea in 2024, and plans matcha and white tea trials for 2025.

Bush and processing

The population stock descends from the palace garden and has no systematic cultivar name. Picking is by hand. Green tea is fixed and pan-fired; black and oolong are separated by oxidation level. Processing happens in the village on simple equipment, which limits volume but allows small, responsive batches. Organic certification through LOAS, BFDA-GHP/GMP and Bhutan Organic underpins the brand. Suja leaf is blended with Loranthus ferrugineus, a mistletoe that grows on Himalayan oaks and gives the brew its pinkish colour.

Tea types and liquor

Green tea has a pale yellow-green liquor with grass, pea and chestnut notes. Black tea is bright red with honey, malt and flowers. Oolong sits between, with fruit, flowers and a light roast. Suja is boiled with butter and salt into a thick, savoury drink central to cold-weather breakfasts and guest hospitality; sweet tea with milk and sugar is common in towns and shops. Local leaf is scarce, and domestic demand still depends heavily on imports, especially brick tea and tea bags.

Industry and market

The cooperative farms about 100 acres and plans to lease another 100 acres of state land. Member income ranges from Nu 40,000 to 700,000 a year depending on land and labour. Green, black and oolong retail at about Nu 180–200 a packet, suja leaf at Nu 120, and loose tea around Nu 3,380 per kg. Output and exports are small. Tourism and the Himalayan organic story are growth points, but brand and capacity are still being built.

Brewing and visiting

Green: 80°C, 2–3 minutes. Black: 90°C, 3 minutes. Oolong: 90°C, short infusions. Suja must be boiled and churned with butter and salt; it is not a plain infusion. March–May and September–November are the most comfortable times to visit Trongsa. The drive from Thimphu is long, and mountain roads and permits need planning. Cooperative visits and picking experiences should be booked ahead.

Misconceptions and limits

Bhutanese tea is all imported ignores the real Samdrupcholing output. Bhutan is an ancient tea country is also an exaggeration: the modern garden is only decades old. Organic certification covers the cooperative, not every smallholder. Suja is a food, not a plain tea. Placing Samdrupcholing on the southeast border is a geographical error; it is in central Trongsa.

How the cooperative works

Samdrupcholing’s gardens are managed in family plots; green leaf goes to a small village processing point, is bought by weight and quality, and is redistributed at the year-end. The 34 members range from 20 to 81, and many are the main labour in their households. In 2019 the cooperative earned over Nu 4 million, with members making roughly Nu 70,000–500,000. After expanding to about 100 acres in 2024, output is still limited by picking labour. The cooperative also sells planting material so neighbouring households can replant.
