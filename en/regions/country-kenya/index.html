Tea in Kenya

Kenya

Kenya is one of Africa’s largest tea exporters. Rift Valley highlands, volcanic soils, two rainy seasons and CTC processing make Kenyan black tea a strength base for global blends. Smallholders, KTDA and the Mombasa auction are the three keys.

The short answer

Kenyan tea is concentrated in the Rift Valley and western highlands: Kericho, Nandi, Limuru, Kisii, Nyeri and Murang’a, at roughly 1,500–2,500 metres. CTC black tea dominates, with small orthodox, green and purple tea. Smallholders grow most of the crop; KTDA provides factories and auction access, and Mombasa is the export hub. Read Kenya as a system of altitude, CTC, smallholders and auction, not a single-estate origin.

Geography and growing conditions

The tea belt runs along the western edge of the East African Rift and the highlands, on fertile volcanic soils with good drainage. Annual rain is about 1,200–2,000 mm, with two rainy seasons (March–May and September–November) and two main flushes. Altitude of 1,500–2,500 metres slows growth and builds strength; strong equatorial sun requires shade and soil conservation. Hail, drought and frost are highland risks, and climate change makes rains less reliable.

The west around Kisii, Nandi and Kericho is wetter; Limuru and Nyeri in the east are cooler, with different flavour. Smallholder plots of 0.5–2 acres deliver leaf to collection points or factories; large estates remain, but smallholders and KTDA factories are the core.

History and industry

Tea arrived around 1903 and commercial planting expanded in the 1920s under colonial estates. After independence in the 1960s, smallholder production expanded with KTDA support, creating a “smallholder + cooperative + factory” structure; Mombasa became the East African auction centre. Since the 1990s, private factories and brands have grown, but KTDA remains the main smallholder channel.

Kenya is now a major black-tea exporter, with output in the hundreds of thousands of tonnes and CTC dominating. Domestic demand is sweet milk tea; exports go to the UK, Pakistan, Egypt and the UAE. Prices follow the Mombasa auction, exchange rates, weather and labour costs, and smallholder returns are volatile. Labour, climate and land fragmentation are long-term issues.

Tea types and flavour

Kenyan tea is mainly CTC black: deep red, strong, malty and woody, suited to tea bags, breakfast tea and milk blends. Orthodox is small and cleaner, with flower, fruit and honey. Green and purple tea are niche; purple leaf is rich in anthocyanins and softer in flavour. Kenyan tea is rarely sold as a single estate; most enters blends.

Highland CTC strength is an advantage but can turn bitter. Brew at 95–100°C for three to four minutes; milk suppresses astringency. Plain-drinkers can choose orthodox or purple tea and shorten the steep.

Cultivars and craft

Kenya plants Assam hybrids and TRFK clones such as TRFK 6/8, 31/8 and 41/8, selected for yield, drought tolerance and quality balance. CTC runs withering, crush–tear–curl, fermentation, drying and sorting; cell rupture gives even fermentation and a strong tea-bag profile. Orthodox keeps whole strips for small specialty lots.

Grades are mainly BP1, PF1, PD and fannings; particle size sets brewing speed. Orthodox is graded by OP, BOP and FTGFOP. Quality depends on altitude, season, factory and batch, not the “Kenya” label alone.

Market and grades

The Mombasa auction is the main export channel. KTDA and smallholder factories sell through the auction, and international buyers blend; private estates and brands also sell direct. Prices follow auction, exchange rates, drought and labour costs, and smallholder income is volatile. Domestic demand is milk tea and tea bags; orthodox specialty is small but growing.

Buy by CTC or orthodox first. CTC is judged by particle, strength and freshness; orthodox by strip, tip and aroma. Brands are usually blends and single estates are rare; factory, batch and season matter more than the country name.

The cup and brewing

Brew CTC at 95–100°C for three to four minutes with milk and sugar, the East African everyday; plain, shorten to two to three minutes to avoid bitterness. Orthodox takes 90–95°C for three minutes; taste for flower, fruit and malt. Purple tea takes 85–90°C and a short steep. Kenyan chai adds milk, sugar and ginger or spices and is central to daily life.

Visit Kenya’s gardens in March–May or September–November; Kericho, Nandi and Limuru offer gardens and factories. The Mombasa auction needs booking; Nairobi has tea shops and brands.

Myths and boundaries

“Kenyan tea is all cheap CTC” fits volume but ignores orthodox, green and purple niche teas. “Kenyan tea equals British breakfast” is a blend story: Kenya is a raw-material supplier, not the only source. “High altitude equals specialty” is false: bulk CTC also comes from highlands.

Kenya’s tea belt is in the Rift Valley and west, not nationwide; administrative borders differ from garden distribution, and auction or brand cannot replace factory and batch. This page gives structure; specific tea pages and producers carry grade and price.
