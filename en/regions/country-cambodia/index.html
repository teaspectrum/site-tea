Tea in Cambodia

Cambodia

Cambodian tea is a 1960s plantation interrupted by war, plus a great deal of imported tea: the old Kirirom garden and Chamkar Te in Mondulkiri are the main domestic memories.

The short answer

Cambodia is not a traditional tea country. Local tea survives in two places: the remains of a 1960s plantation in Kirirom National Park, where the local Camellia sinensis var. cambodiensis still grows, and smallholder or community plots around Chamkar Te in Mondulkiri. National consumption is import-led, mostly green tea, black tea and milk-tea ingredients; about 1,050 t was imported in 2024, far above local output.

Geography and growing conditions

Kirirom sits on a plateau in Kampong Speu at about 700 m, with pine forest, waterfalls and cooler air. Mondulkiri is a northeastern highland at 200–800 m, with red soils, forest and a long rainy season. Tea likes highland acid soils and cloud, but the dry season brings water shortages and the wet season turns red roads into mud. The hot plains suit rice, rubber and cashew, not commercial tea.

History and industry

Tea is often said to have reached the Khmer court with Chinese traders, but no industry formed. In the 1960s King Norodom Sihanouk established a tea plantation at Kirirom as an import-substitution project. War and the Khmer Rouge era abandoned the gardens and destroyed roads and processing equipment. After the 1990s, small recovery projects appeared at Kirirom and Mondulkiri. From the 2000s, urban milk tea and iced tea expanded, but imported material supplied most of it. Today's Cambodia Tea Renaissance is still a small revival project, not a mature industry.

Bush and processing

Kirirom keeps the local Camellia sinensis var. cambodiensis, whose leaf and flavour differ from standard commercial cultivars; Mondulkiri plots use China- and Vietnam-derived populations. Smallholders pick by hand and pan-fire green tea, with small black and oolong batches. Equipment is simple, withering and oxidation depend on weather, and batch variation is wide. There is no large CTC factory and no stable export chain.

Tea types and liquor

Local green tea has a pale yellow-green liquor with grass, nut and light smoke. Black tea is medium in strength, with wood and honey; oolong is made in very small lots. Urban drinking is sweet iced tea, lemon tea and condensed-milk milk tea, where sugar and milk often mask the leaf. Khmer tee usually means an iced, sweetened drink, not a plain infusion. Imported green and black tea bags are the everyday base.

Industry and market

Local output is small; the Kirirom remnant and Mondulkiri communities are the main sources. Imports reached about 1.05k t and US$4.52 million in 2024, mainly from Vietnam, China and Thailand. Brands and certification are weak, and prices range from community green tea to imported bags. Tourism and the Kirirom old-garden story add premium. Area figures for the remnant also reflect the rupture in surveying and land title after the war; there is no unified national tea statistics, so any output number is an estimate. Mondulkiri community projects are tied to NGOs and tourism, and leaf volumes are still too small for an independent brand. Area figures for the remnant vary, so no single number should be treated as the national tea area.

Brewing and visiting

Local green: 80–85°C, 2 minutes. Black: 90–95°C, 3 minutes. Oolong: 90°C, short infusions. Kirirom is about 2–3 hours from Phnom Penh and most stable in the November–April dry season. Mondulkiri is 6–8 hours by road and needs a 4x4 in the wet season. Community garden visits go through NGOs or cooperatives.

Misconceptions and limits

Cambodia has no tea is wrong, but Cambodia has ancient tea forests is also wrong. Kirirom is a modern 1960s plantation remnant, not a thousand-year-old garden; most Mondulkiri planting is recent. Do not apply wild-tea or old-tree labels to Cambodia, and do not mistake imported milk tea for local tea.

Kirirom’s old garden and Mondulkiri’s new plots

The old Kirirom garden survives as scattered rows among pine forest and tourist buildings. The local Camellia sinensis var. cambodiensis differs from mainstream cultivars in leaf and aroma, but yields are low and processing is dispersed. Around Chamkar Te and O’Raing in Mondulkiri, recent trials have planted plots of about 10 ha using cuttings from local old bushes. The Cambodia Tea Renaissance project wants tea to sit beside Kampot pepper as a signature national crop, but nurseries, processing and export standards are still at the beginning.

The nursery and processing gap

Cambodia has no commercial tea nursery; community expansion relies on layering and cuttings from old local bushes, with uneven survival and uniformity. Processing points lack withering troughs and rolling machines, and wet-season leaf spoils easily. A stable export lot depends first on nurseries, roads and a standard small processing line, not on branding.

Source boundary

Cambodia is a net tea importer. Local tea survives at the old Kirirom plantation and a few Mondulkiri communities, and area figures differ: the remnant is often cited at about 300 ha, while one field study records about 1,500 ha of tea inside the park. The 1960s planting, 1970s war and post-1990s revival are the key markers; this is not a thousand-year-old tea forest.
