Guangxi's tea map has three lines: aged Liubao in Wuzhou, jasmine scenting in Hengzhou, and mountain oil tea and green tea in the north.

The short answer

Guangxi is not a single-category region. Wuzhou Liubao is a dark tea known for ageing; Hengzhou (formerly Hengxian) is a major jasmine flower and scenting base; Guilin, Liuzhou and Hezhou produce green tea, osmanthus tea and mountain oil tea. Read Guangxi by asking “Liubao, jasmine or mountain green?” — the raw material, process and market are different worlds.

Geography and growing conditions

Guangxi is low-latitude subtropical, with hills and karst mountains, humid heat, abundant rain and dense rivers. Wuzhou sits at the confluence of the Xun and Gui rivers, a historical West River shipping hub that made Liubao export possible. Hengzhou lies at the edge of the Nanning basin; jasmine growing and scenting need abundant flowers and hot summers. Northern Guangxi is higher, with tea gardens interwoven with oil-tea woods and bamboo; spring arrives early but late cold snaps can hurt quality.

Terroir and craft

Guangxi tea is spread widely: Wuzhou Liubao is known for aged dark tea, Hengxian (now Hengzhou) is a national jasmine core, and Guilin, Sanjiang and Zhaoping make green and black tea. Local populations, Yunnan large-leaf, Fuding and jasmine are used. Liubao is fixed, rolled, pile-fermented and aged; jasmine tea is scented. The craft range is broad.

History and industry

Liubao was shipped down the West River to the Pearl River Delta and Southeast Asia in the Qing era, taking its name from Liubao town in Wuzhou. In the mid-twentieth century, state factories and overseas-Chinese export channels formalised production; after 2000, the “older is better” narrative drove an aged-tea market. Hengzhou jasmine scenting scaled up in the 1970s–80s and became a national flower-tea processing base. Guilin osmanthus tea combines local flowers with green or black tea.

Tea types and everyday drinking

Liubao is a dark tea, traditionally basket-packed or loose, with red-brown liquor, betel-nut and aged aromas, suited to boiling and long storage. Hengzhou jasmine tea uses a green-tea base scented repeatedly; the flower aroma should be fresh and lively. Northern Guangxi oil tea is not an infusion but a food: tea, ginger, garlic and peanuts are pounded and boiled, served with puffed rice and fried dough. Guilin osmanthus tea is a scented or blended flower tea.

Industry and market

Guangxi produces a moderate volume. Liubao and Hengxian jasmine are the core brands for domestic, Southeast Asian and export markets. Aged Liubao and gift boxes are expensive; jasmine ranges from everyday tea bags to fine scenting. Guilin and Sanjiang green tea have weaker brands and friendlier prices.

The cup and brewing

Liubao is deep red, with betel-nut, wood and aged aroma, a full body and finish. Hengxian jasmine is yellow-green, with fresh floral lift and sweetness; Guilin green is lighter. Brew Liubao at 95–100°C or boil; jasmine at 85–90°C. Visit Wuzhou and Hengzhou from May to September, with jasmine blooming in summer.

Buying and boundaries

For Liubao, check leaf grade, fermentation, storage and absence of mould; aged aroma is not the same as warehouse smell. For jasmine tea, check scenting rounds, natural flower aroma and fresh base; flavoured tea smells loud but fades quickly. Oil tea is food, not a premium-leaf contest. Brew Liubao at 100°C or boil it; jasmine at 85–90°C with quick pours; do not stew osmanthus tea.

Myths and boundaries

“Guangxi only has Liubao” ignores Hengxian jasmine, Guilin green and Sanjiang black tea. Guangxi is a multi-category origin with dark, scented and green tea. Nor is “Liubao always improves with age”: storage and craft decide aging quality.

Source boundary

Guangxi spans several climate zones; “Guangxi tea” is not one label. Liubao history is tied to overseas-Chinese trade and river shipping, with large differences between factories and years. Hengzhou's base status refers to flowers and scenting, not to every packet sold as jasmine tea.
