Pesticide Residues

Guide

An MRL is a maximum residue limit, not a zero-residue rule. Testing dry leaf and estimating exposure from the brew are two different steps. Standards, transfer factors and brewing practice matter more than the slogan “is there pesticide residue?”.

“Tea contains pesticide residue” and “this cup exceeds a safety limit” are not the same finding. Tea residues are usually measured in dry leaf, while people drink the infusion. From dry leaf to brew, transfer rates vary widely between pesticides. Understanding the MRL, the test matrix and the transfer factor turns “detected” into an interpretable exposure.

What an MRL is — and is not

A Maximum Residue Limit (MRL) is the highest residue level allowed in a agricultural product or food under a specific regulatory framework, calculated for a defined test matrix. For tea, MRLs are usually expressed on dry leaf.

An MRL is not a synonym for “safe line”, and it is not “zero residue”:

  • Not zero: residues within the limit are allowed; detection is not automatically a violation.
  • Not a toxicity threshold: the MRL is a regulatory tool. Consumer risk assessment also considers intake, transfer and population.
  • Not globally uniform: China's GB 2763, EU Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, Japan's positive list and US EPA tolerances have different lists and limits.

So when a report says “this tea exceeded the pesticide limit”, first ask: which standard, which pesticide, dry leaf or brew, and what detection limit?

From fresh leaf to brew: residues change, but do not disappear

Stage What can happen Effect on exposure
Fresh leaf Field application, degradation, rain washing Sets the starting residue
Processing Withering, fixation, rolling, drying, roasting Some pesticides volatilise, degrade or concentrate
Made tea The matrix for testing and MRLs Residue expressed on dry leaf
Brewing Transfer into the infusion Transfer factor determines the ingested amount
Spent leaf Residue not extracted Usually not eaten

The EU reference laboratories provide a key concept: transfer factor = pesticide amount in brew / pesticide amount in dry tea. They collected 1,206 transfer factors for 108 pesticides. Most transfer rates are below 100%; lipophilic compounds (Log P > 4) are usually lower, and 20% can serve as a conservative approximation. In EFSA's fenazaquin assessment, the median processing factor for green tea infusion was about 0.29, again separating dry-leaf residue from brew exposure.

Detection, exceedance and risk

A 2024 study of 64 ordinary and herbal tea samples found residues in 47%, with 2% above the EU MRL. Most pesticides transferred more in a 5-minute brew than in a 30-minute brew. The highest acute risk was linuron in mint for children, at 17% of the ARfD. These numbers suggest:

  • detection is common, exceedance is rare;
  • exceedance is not automatically a health risk; transfer, intake and population matter;
  • herbal teas (mint, lemon balm) have different pesticide lists and dietary exposure from Camellia tea and should not be lumped together.

Chinese and EU frameworks

China's GB 2763 sets maximum residue limits for many pesticides in tea, expressed on dry tea. The EU MRL regulation (EC 396/2005) also uses dry tea, but the lists and limits differ. EFSA import-tolerance assessments calculate crop, processing factor and dietary exposure, which is why the same pesticide can appear as 3 mg/kg in one jurisdiction and 9 mg/kg in another.

This is not simply “who is stricter”. Lists, analytical methods, diets and trade requirements all differ. For a consumer, the relevant question is whether the product meets the rules of the market where it is sold, not whether it matches a limit from another jurisdiction.

Organic does not mean zero residue

“Organic” usually means the production and processing comply with an organic standard that restricts synthetic pesticides and fertilisers. It does not mean every residue is undetectable, and it does not cover heavy metals or mycotoxins. Organic tea fields can still have background contamination, drift from neighbouring fields, and processing or storage contamination. Treating “organic” as “zero pesticide” is misleading.

What a household can do

  1. Check the standard and the matrix. Does the report test dry leaf or brew, and what is the method's detection limit?
  2. Do not chase “zero residue”. Compliant products may contain residues within the limit; “not detected” can simply reflect a high detection limit.
  3. Shorten the brew. Some pesticides transfer more in 5 minutes than in 30 minutes, but this is not a universal solution.
  4. Do not rely on rinsing. A brief warm rinse can remove surface dust and some soluble material, but has limited effect on residues inside the leaf.
  5. Diversify. Rotating origins, tea types and batches is more practical than fixating on one brand.

Source boundary

This page can establish: MRLs are expressed on dry tea and are neither a zero-residue rule nor a toxicity threshold; brew exposure requires a transfer factor; transfer differs by pesticide, tea type and brew time; Chinese GB 2763 and EU MRL lists and limits differ; organic certification is not zero residue.

This page cannot establish: the residue level of a specific brand, batch or origin; whether a particular test report is compliant; the exact risk of one cup for one person. For a specific product, consult the rules of the selling market, the test report and regulatory notices. If a product smells unusual, is mouldy or looks suspicious, use the local food-safety complaint and testing channels.
