Sensory Science of Tea

Guide

Tasting tea is more than “I like it / I do not”. Aroma, taste, mouthfeel and finish arise from different molecules and receptors. Control water, leaf ratio, time and evaluation order to turn a personal impression into a comparable description.

Tea tasting falls into two traps: treating “I like it” as “it is good”, or reciting “orchid, rock rhyme, throat rhyme” without explaining what was perceived. Sensory science does not score the tea for you. It offers a repeatable method: control the brew, separate aroma, taste, mouthfeel and finish, then describe differences with words that can be checked.

Four things happen at once

Channel What you perceive Main sources
Aroma Dry leaf, wet leaf, liquor, empty cup Volatile terpenes, aldehydes, alcohols, esters, pyrazines, lactones
Taste Sweet, bitter, umami, sour, salty Sugars, caffeine, catechins, amino acids, organic acids, salts
Mouthfeel Thick, thin, silky, astringent, drying, grainy Polyphenol–protein binding, pectin, fibre, suspended particles
Finish Sweet aftertaste, lingering aroma, dryness, bitter tail Saliva and oral proteins, retained volatiles, post-swallow receptors

Aroma and taste are often conflated because smell has two routes: orthonasal (sniffing) and retronasal (aroma travelling from the mouth to the nasal cavity during swallowing). A tea that “tastes floral” may be giving a floral impression through retronasal olfaction, not the tongue.

Taste: astringency is not bitterness

Tea taste research separates the channels:

  • Astringency: flavonol-O-glycosides, tannins and galloylated catechins bind salivary proteins, producing drying, tightening and roughness.
  • Bitterness: caffeine and non-galloylated catechins activate T2R bitter receptors.
  • Umami: L-theanine, succinic acid, gallic acid and theogallin contribute umami and a sweet-savoury impression.
  • Sweet aftertaste: in green tea, linked to hydrolysis of galloylated catechins and changes in saliva and the oral environment.

This is why “bitter and astringent” is too coarse: bitterness is a receptor signal; astringency is tactile and protein-binding. A tea can be bitter without being astringent, or astringent without being bitter.

Mouthfeel: the structure of the liquid

Mouthfeel is not taste intensity. Light, thin, silky, thick, creamy, coating, astringent and grainy describe physical and chemical contact with the mouth. Thickness may come from pectin, soluble sugars, amino acids and some oxidation products; astringency comes from polyphenol–protein binding.

Roasting changes this structure. In Wuyi Rougui, deeper roasting increased thickness and intensity, but excessive roasting raised bitterness and sourness; sweet aftertaste, umami and briskness peaked at different stages. Processing is not “more is better”; it balances several sensory dimensions.

Turn impressions into comparable notes

  1. Fix the brew. Same water, temperature, leaf-to-water ratio, time and vessel. Follow the logic of ISO 3103: control variables before comparing.
  2. Evaluate in order. Dry aroma → wet aroma → liquor colour → taste → mouthfeel → finish. Do not conclude on the first sip.
  3. Use a reference. Comparing two teas on the same day reveals differences better than a single tea alone.
  4. Separate intensity from liking. “Strong aroma” and “I like this aroma” are two judgments.
  5. Record reproducible words. Floral, roasted, woody, fruity acidity, umami, astringent, sweet aftertaste; avoid unverifiable “energy” or “qi”.

Common biases

Bias Manifestation Correction
Order effect The first tea shapes the next Randomise order, rinse with water, rest between
Expectation Price or origin label makes it taste better Blind tasting; reveal the label after
Temperature Hot and warm tea differ in aroma and bitterness Fix tasting temperature; note changes as it cools
Vocabulary inflation Terms replace observation Add “what I smelled / where I felt it” after each term
Concentration as quality “Stronger” equals “better” Separate intensity, complexity, balance and finish

Source boundary

This page can establish: tea taste involves multiple molecules and receptors; astringency and bitterness have different mechanisms; aroma includes orthonasal and retronasal routes; mouthfeel relates to pectin and polyphenols; standardised brewing and blind tasting reduce bias.

This page cannot establish: that a specific tea must show a specific aroma or taste; that one term maps to one fixed molecule; that personal preference can be replaced by “science”. Sensory evaluation remains a human judgment; method only makes it more repeatable and discussable.

Where Tea Aroma Comes From

Tea aroma is not one molecule but a shifting mix of volatiles shaped by cultivar, processing and brewing. Learn to separate grassy, floral, fruity, sweet and roasted notes before asking whether a tea is merely “aromatic”.

Amino Acids and Umami

Tea umami comes from free amino acids balanced against polyphenols, caffeine and sugars — not from a “MSG taste”. L-theanine is one important player, but the freshness of a cup is a product of the mixture, cultivar, shading and brewing.

Polyphenols and Astringency

Astringency is not bitterness, and it is not a simple “more polyphenols” score. It is a drying, rough, tightening sensation produced when polyphenols interact with salivary proteins and the oral surface; concentration, composition, saliva and brewing all change its strength.
