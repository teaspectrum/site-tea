Tea and Iron Absorption

Guide

Tea polyphenols and tannins can bind iron and mainly reduce non-haem iron absorption. The size of the effect depends on timing, iron source and the whole meal. One cup is not the sole cause of anaemia; people at risk should test first and adjust second.

“Tea causes anaemia” is a common shortcut. A more accurate statement: tea polyphenols and tannins can bind iron and mainly reduce non-haem iron absorption. The size of the effect depends on when tea is drunk, the iron source and the rest of the meal. For most healthy people, normal tea drinking is not the sole cause of iron deficiency; for high-risk groups, timing and pairing are worth adjusting.

Two kinds of iron first

Iron type Main sources Effect of tea
Haem iron Red meat, liver, blood products Relatively small
Non-haem iron Plants, legumes, grains, iron supplements Relatively large

Tea polyphenols and tannins readily form complexes with ferric iron and reduce its absorption in the gut. Haem iron uses a different pathway and is less affected. This is why vegetarians and people taking iron supplements need to pay more attention to timing.

Timing matters more than “tea or no tea”

  • Tea with a meal: strongest inhibition of non-haem iron. Polyphenols and iron meet in the gut at the same time.
  • Tea between meals: usually a smaller effect. Once the iron absorption window has passed, polyphenol interference falls.
  • Strong tea with an iron supplement on an empty stomach: the least useful combination. The supplement is already limited; strong tea can reduce absorption further.

An easy arrangement: put tea between meals, and wait 1–2 hours after an iron supplement or an iron-rich meal. Not everyone must do this, but people who are already low or supplementing should consider it first.

The whole meal changes the result

A meal contains more than tea. These factors also affect non-haem iron absorption:

  • Vitamin C: promotes non-haem iron absorption and can partly offset tea's inhibition.
  • Meat, fish and poultry: contain a “meat factor” that promotes non-haem iron absorption.
  • Phytate, calcium and coffee: also inhibit iron absorption; tea is not the only variable.
  • Iron stores and need: people with low iron absorb more efficiently; healthy people absorb less. Individual status matters more than one cup.

So “how much does one cup reduce absorption” cannot be answered without the meal, iron type and personal status.

Who should pay attention

  1. Women with heavy menstrual bleeding: higher iron loss and need.
  2. Pregnancy and breastfeeding: higher iron demand; deficiency is common in pregnancy.
  3. Children and adolescents: growth requires iron, and diets may be narrow.
  4. Vegetarians and people who eat little meat: non-haem iron dominates, so absorption efficiency matters more.
  5. Blood donors and people with diagnosed iron deficiency: low stores make the supplement period more sensitive.

These groups do not need to give up tea. They can move strong tea away from the main meal and iron supplement, and ensure enough total iron, protein and vitamin C.

Do not self-diagnose by “stopping tea”

Anaemia has many causes: low intake, malabsorption, chronic blood loss, heavy periods, pregnancy demand, disease and medication. “I drink tea” cannot explain it, and “stop tea” cannot treat it. Persistent fatigue, palpitations, pallor, dizziness or reduced exercise tolerance should prompt a blood count and ferritin test, with a clinician's interpretation.

Four common misreadings

Claim Problem More defensible reading
“Tea always causes anaemia” Tea mainly affects non-haem iron, and timing and dose matter Normal tea drinking is usually not the sole cause in healthy people
“Black tea is worse than green tea” Polyphenol profiles differ, but strength and amount matter more Look at concentration, cups and timing
“No tea at all while supplementing iron” Not an absolute ban; separate the timing A 1–2 hour gap from strong tea is more sensible
“Stop tea and anaemia improves” Anaemia has many causes; stopping tea is not treatment Test first, then treat the cause

Source boundary

This page supports: tea polyphenols/tannins mainly inhibit non-haem iron absorption and affect haem iron less; tea with a meal usually inhibits more than tea between meals; vitamin C, meat, fish and poultry promote non-haem iron absorption; heavy menstrual bleeding, pregnancy, children, vegetarians, blood donors and iron-deficient people need more attention; iron need and stores change absorption efficiency.

This page does not support: the exact change in a person's iron markers after tea; one consistent effect across all tea types and strengths; tea as the sole cause of anaemia or “stop tea” as treatment. High-risk people should combine blood count, ferritin and dietary assessment with a clinician or dietitian.

Catechins

Catechins are the polyphenols most closely associated with green tea. EGCG makes up roughly half to 70% of the total; astringency comes from its interaction with salivary proteins; processing oxidises the same molecules into theaflavins and thearubigins; health claims must return to dose and evidence.

Reading Tea Health Evidence

A tea-health headline is not a conclusion. Start with the study design, the population, the dose and the outcome before deciding how far the claim can travel.

Tea and Sleep

Tea affects sleep mainly through caffeine dose, timing and individual metabolism—not through the word “tea”. Falling asleep is not the same as sleeping well, and a time window is more useful than a fixed “no tea after X”.
