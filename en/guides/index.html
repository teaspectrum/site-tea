A Guide to Water for Tea

Water is neither “the purer the better” nor “the more expensive the mineral water the better”. TDS, hardness, pH and residual chlorine change extraction, liquor colour and aroma. For most homes, low-to-moderate TDS, low hardness, no chlorine and near-neutral pH is a stable starting point.

Amino Acids and Umami

Tea umami comes from free amino acids balanced against polyphenols, caffeine and sugars — not from a “MSG taste”. L-theanine is one important player, but the freshness of a cup is a product of the mixture, cultivar, shading and brewing.

Brewing Troubleshooting

When tea tastes wrong, the leaf is not always to blame. Bitterness, thinness, cloudiness, lost aroma, sourness and mustiness usually map to water, temperature, leaf ratio, time, vessel or storage. Work symptom by symptom.

Caffeine

Caffeine in tea is a dose question before it is a tea-type question. Estimate the cup from serving size, leaf quantity, leaf form and steeping time, then keep the EFSA, FDA and pregnancy boundaries separate.

Caffeine in a Cup

Caffeine in a cup is not a fixed number. It is leaf amount × caffeine content × extraction rate, then modified by tea type, leaf grade, water temperature, time and infusion number. An estimate is more useful than memorising a table.

Catechins

Catechins are the polyphenols most closely associated with green tea. EGCG makes up roughly half to 70% of the total; astringency comes from its interaction with salivary proteins; processing oxidises the same molecules into theaflavins and thearubigins; health claims must return to dose and evidence.

Fluoride in Tea

Tea plants take fluoride from the soil, and mature leaves and brick tea usually contain more than young-leaf tea. The risk is not “fluoride exists in tea”, but long-term large-volume drinking of high-fluoride brick tea combined with water and other sources.

L-Theanine

L-theanine is a non-protein amino acid characteristic of tea and contributes to umami, sweetness and body. It crosses the blood-brain barrier, but human evidence for relaxation, sleep and focus is inconsistent; a cup of tea cannot be equated with a supplement dose.

Microbes in Tea

Dry tea is not sterile. Bacillus, Aspergillus, Penicillium and yeasts are common from processing and storage; most detections do not mean disease, but humid storage raises mould and toxin risk. Ask what microbe, how many, and whether the tea stayed dry.

Oxidation Claims

Scavenging free radicals in a test tube does not mean protecting human cells after drinking. EFSA separates “radical-scavenging capacity in a model system” from “a substantiated human health effect”; ORAC and similar in vitro indices cannot stand alone.

An MRL is a maximum residue limit, not a zero-residue rule. Testing dry leaf and estimating exposure from the brew are two different steps. Standards, transfer factors and brewing practice matter more than the slogan “is there pesticide residue?”.

Polyphenols and Astringency

Astringency is not bitterness, and it is not a simple “more polyphenols” score. It is a drying, rough, tightening sensation produced when polyphenols interact with salivary proteins and the oral surface; concentration, composition, saliva and brewing all change its strength.

Shade, Altitude and Flavour

Shade and altitude both change fresh-leaf chemistry, but neither is a simple "more shade, more sweetness" or "higher altitude, more aroma" switch. Shade shifts photosynthesis and nitrogen metabolism; altitude shifts temperature, light and growth pace. Cultivar, season and processing keep pulling at the result.

Storage Chemistry

Storing tea is not “the older the better”. Moisture, oxygen, temperature, light and residual enzyme activity change aroma, liquor colour and taste. Green, oolong, black and post-fermented teas age differently; home storage starts with moisture, oxygen, light and odour control.

Tea and Drug Interactions

Most tea–drug interactions are not about a single cup. They involve long-term high intake, strong brews or concentrated tea extracts changing drug absorption or metabolism. Separate the component, the evidence and the timing before deciding whether to change anything.

Tea and Iron Absorption

Tea polyphenols and tannins can bind iron and mainly reduce non-haem iron absorption. The size of the effect depends on timing, iron source and the whole meal. One cup is not the sole cause of anaemia; people at risk should test first and adjust second.

Tea and Sleep

Tea affects sleep mainly through caffeine dose, timing and individual metabolism—not through the word “tea”. Falling asleep is not the same as sleeping well, and a time window is more useful than a fixed “no tea after X”.
