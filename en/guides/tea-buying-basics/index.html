Tea Buying Basics

Guide

Buying tea is not memorising grade acronyms or chasing a famous origin. Decide what kind of tea you want, then check dry leaf, aroma, processing, date, packaging and a trial brew. Grade letters describe leaf size and tip content, not the quality in the cup.

The most common buying mistake is treating information as quality: longer grade letters, a famous origin, a higher price. These signals help, but none decides the cup alone. A better method is to decide what you want, then verify with dry leaf, aroma, processing, date, packaging and a trial brew.

Start with the need

Need Look first at Do not over-weight
Daily drinking Freshness, consistency, price, stamina Rare grades, long stories
Gift Packaging integrity, traceability, batch notes Over-packaging, unverifiable “master” claims
Exploration Samples, processing character, origin differences Bulk jars
Long storage Moisture, processing, storage conditions, raw material “All tea gets better with age”
Blending / milk tea Strength, aroma, price Premium whole-leaf grades

Buying a sample is the cheapest way to avoid a bad bulk purchase. Ten grams and 500 grams carry very different risk.

What grade letters actually say

Black tea trade grades such as OP, FOP, GFOP, TGFOP and FTGFOP mainly describe leaf size, wholeness and tip content. CTC grades such as BP1, PF1, PD and Dust describe particle size. They describe physical appearance and sorting, not a flavour score.

  • OP: Orange Pekoe, longer whole leaf, usually without tips.
  • FOP: Flowery Orange Pekoe, some young leaf and tips; “Flowery” refers to plucking, not floral taste.
  • TGFOP / FTGFOP: Higher tip content and finer sorting; the numeral 1 often marks a higher grade within the batch.
  • CTC: Crush–Tear–Curl, granular, fast-extracting and strong, common in tea bags and milk tea.

A higher grade does not mean you will like it more. Some prefer the clarity of FTGFOP; others want the strength of CTC. Grade tells you what form you have, not whether it is good.

What dry leaf can show

  1. Natural colour. A batch should be relatively even. Oily shine, fluorescent brightness or greyish white may suggest processing or storage issues.
  2. Leaf integrity. Whole-leaf tea should be tightly rolled; a broken tea bag is not whole leaf, so do not apply the same standard.
  3. Clumping and moisture. It should snap, not feel soft or clumped.
  4. Clean aroma. Tea aroma, not mustiness, smoke off-notes, perfume or detergent.
  5. Excess powder. A little is normal; a lot affects brewing and clarity.

Aroma and trial brew

Dry aroma is only the first layer. Brew with one fixed set of parameters: same water, temperature, leaf-to-water ratio, time and vessel. Follow the logic of ISO 3103: control variables before comparing. Taste at least two infusions and check:

  • hot, warm and empty-cup aroma;
  • liquor clarity and brightness;
  • bitterness, astringency, sourness, smoke or mustiness;
  • mouthfeel and finish;
  • whether the second and third infusions collapse quickly.

A tea that is fragrant only in the first infusion may have surface aroma or incomplete processing; but check whether your water and timing are the real cause.

Date, packaging and source

  • Production / packing date. Green and light oolong teas are best fresh; black and roasted oolong teas need some rest after roasting; post-fermented tea needs a storage record.
  • Packaging. Opaque, sealed and moisture-resistant first; clear bags and paper bags are poor for long storage.
  • Source. A seller who can explain origin, cultivar, processing and batch is more checkable than one who only says “old tree / master / core region”.
  • Testing. Pesticide, heavy-metal and microbiological reports should show matrix and date, not just a “pass” stamp.
  • Price. It may reflect raw material, processing, rarity, packaging or brand; it does not equal your preference.

Four traps

Trap Problem Response
“Longer grade is better” Grade describes appearance, not flavour Taste first; grade as reference
“Core region is always better” Variation within a region is large; batch and processing matter Check batch and taste
“Old tea gets better” Most tea deteriorates; damp storage moulds Store only suitable tea; control moisture and oxygen
“Organic / zero residue = absolutely safe” Organic mainly regulates production; not zero residue Check standard, matrix and source

A workable buying sequence

  1. Set type and budget. Decide green / oolong / black / white / post-fermented first, so the shelf does not decide for you.
  2. Buy samples. Compare at least 2–3, recording brew parameters.
  3. Taste blind once. Taste before seeing price and label to reduce expectation bias.
  4. Check dry leaf and packaging. Damp, musty or clumped tea is out.
  5. Confirm date and storage. Ask production/packing date and recommended storage.
  6. Re-buy small. Buy a larger pack only after you like it; do not stock a year on the first purchase.

Source boundary

This page can establish: black tea grades mainly describe leaf size, wholeness and tip content; orthodox and CTC are different form systems; dry-leaf colour, structure, aroma, moisture and packaging are useful buying signals; standardised brewing and blind tasting reduce comparison bias.

This page cannot establish: that one grade always tastes better; that one origin or brand is always superior; a fixed price-to-quality ratio. Judge by trial, batch, testing and storage, not a single label.

Tea Tasting Vocabulary

Tasting vocabulary is not a contest in mysticism. A good description points to a specific object and intensity: dry leaf, liquor, aroma, taste, mouthfeel and infused leaf. Terms such as “orchid” or “rock rhyme” must be unpacked into observable cues.

Storage Chemistry

Storing tea is not “the older the better”. Moisture, oxygen, temperature, light and residual enzyme activity change aroma, liquor colour and taste. Green, oolong, black and post-fermented teas age differently; home storage starts with moisture, oxygen, light and odour control.
