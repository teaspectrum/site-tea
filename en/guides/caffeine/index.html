Caffeine

Guide

Caffeine in tea is a dose question before it is a tea-type question. Estimate the cup from serving size, leaf quantity, leaf form and steeping time, then keep the EFSA, FDA and pregnancy boundaries separate.

Two cups of tea can differ by a factor of several. Tea type, origin and grade offer clues, but the dose in the cup is driven mainly by how much leaf you use, how broken the leaf is, how hot the water is, how long you steep, and whether you drink the infusion or the whole leaf.

Set “energy” aside for a moment. Caffeine has a clear dose boundary. A small amount can improve alertness; a dose close to bedtime can disturb sleep; too much can cause palpitations, anxiety and stomach upset, and in extreme cases toxicity. Tea is usually gentler than coffee, but “gentler” does not mean unlimited.

Start with milligrams, not tea types

The commonest error is cup size. Eight ounces (about 240 ml) and twelve ounces (about 355 ml) are not the same cup, and a mug, an office cup and a small gongfu cup are different again. These figures are starting points, not measurements of your cup:

Drink (8 oz / about 240 ml) Typical caffeine Note
Brewed green tea about 29 mg Mayo Clinic reference value
Brewed black tea about 48 mg Usually a little higher than green tea at the same cup size
Brewed coffee about 95 mg About twice the black-tea figure
Decaf black tea about 2 mg Not zero, but very low

A 12 oz serving scales up, but the actual concentration still depends on leaf size and steeping. Do not apply an 8 oz table to a 16 oz mug, and do not treat a tea-type average as the label on a specific bag.

Tea type is not a dose table

“Green tea is low, black tea is high” is true only as a broad average. Real cups have more variables:

  • Leaf form. Broken leaf, tea-bag material and CTC black tea release caffeine quickly; whole leaf usually releases more slowly.
  • Leaf quantity. The difference between 3 g and 6 g is often larger than the difference between green and black tea.
  • Temperature and time. Hotter water and longer steeping extract more caffeine, though concentration does not rise indefinitely.
  • Plucking standard. Buds and older leaves differ; cultivar, season and shading also change caffeine in the dry leaf.
  • Whole-leaf powder. Matcha drinks the whole leaf. Its caffeine cannot be estimated as if the leaf were discarded after steeping.

“Is this cup high in caffeine?” is therefore a better question than “Is this green or black?” Look at leaf quantity, cup size and steeping first.

What caffeine does in the body

Caffeine is structurally close to adenosine and temporarily blocks adenosine receptors. That reduces the feeling of sleepiness and can speed reaction time. It does not remove fatigue or restore lost sleep.

Common subjective effects include:

  • a short-term rise in alertness and attention;
  • faster heartbeat, sweating, tremor or anxiety, especially on an empty stomach, at higher doses or in sensitive people;
  • longer sleep latency and lighter sleep, particularly when drunk close to bedtime;
  • a short-term rise in blood pressure; people with hypertension or arrhythmia should be more cautious.

EFSA’s public explanation notes that 100 mg of caffeine may affect sleep duration and pattern in some adults when consumed close to bedtime. That is lower than many people’s “one cup in the afternoon”, so “I sleep fine” describes your individual response, not a general rule.

Boundaries by group

Group Reference boundary How to read it
Healthy adults Up to 400 mg a day; up to 200 mg in a single dose EFSA and FDA both cite 400 mg a day as a level not generally associated with negative effects; it is not a target
Pregnancy Up to 200 mg a day EFSA considers this level not a safety concern for the fetus; individual advice still belongs with the clinician
Lactation Up to 200 mg in a single dose and per day EFSA considers this level not a safety concern for the breastfed infant; watch the infant’s response
Children and adolescents Insufficient data for a safe intake EFSA can only extrapolate from the adult 3 mg/kg single-dose level; the AAP advises that children avoid caffeine
Anxiety, arrhythmia, reflux, insomnia Individual and usually lower Not a yes/no question, but a relationship between dose, timing and symptoms

Keep tea separate from pure caffeine products. The FDA warns that pure caffeine powder and highly concentrated products can reach toxic doses very quickly; around 1,200 mg may produce seizures and other toxicity. Tea infusion is dilute; that does not make powders, energy drinks or supplements low-risk.

Estimating a cup

A rough frame is:

[ \text{intake} \approx \text{leaf grams} \times \text{caffeine in dry leaf} \times \text{extraction fraction} ]

None of the three terms is fixed. Caffeine in dry leaf varies with cultivar, plucking part, season and shading; extraction varies with leaf size, temperature, time and leaf-to-water ratio. The formula tells you the order of magnitude, not a milligram label.

A more useful method is to record. Fix the cup and leaf quantity, change only steeping time; then fix time and change only leaf quantity. Note palpitations, stomach discomfort and how long it takes to fall asleep. Two or three records teach you more than a table of averages.

Four common myths

Claim Problem More accurate reading
“Green tea always has less caffeine than black” A tea-type average does not describe a single cup Look at leaf quantity, leaf form and steeping; broken black tea or matcha can exceed whole-leaf green tea
“Rinsing the leaf before the first infusion removes caffeine” A quick rinse removes some caffeine, but also aroma and flavour It is not a reliable decaffeination method; choose decaffeinated product or control dose
“Stronger tea always means more caffeine” Concentration is higher, but total intake also depends on how much you drink A small strong cup can contain less than a large weak cup; consider both concentration and volume
“Decaf means caffeine-free” Decaffeinated tea can retain a small amount Decaf black tea is commonly about 2 mg per 8 oz; still not zero for the highly sensitive

Source boundary

This page can confirm: EFSA and FDA cite 400 mg a day and 200 mg in a single dose for healthy adults; 200 mg a day during pregnancy; 100 mg close to bedtime may affect sleep in some adults; an 8 oz cup of brewed green tea contains about 29 mg, black tea about 48 mg and coffee about 95 mg; pure caffeine powder carries a risk of acute toxicity.

This page does not confirm: the caffeine content of a specific bag of tea; a “safe number of cups” for an individual; a medical use of caffeine for a specific disease; or the claim that tea always energises without affecting sleep. Anyone taking medication, pregnant or breastfeeding, or managing a cardiovascular or mental-health condition should place clinical advice above this page.

L-Theanine

L-theanine is a non-protein amino acid characteristic of tea and contributes to umami, sweetness and body. It crosses the blood-brain barrier, but human evidence for relaxation, sleep and focus is inconsistent; a cup of tea cannot be equated with a supplement dose.
