A Guide to Water for Tea

Guide

Water is neither “the purer the better” nor “the more expensive the mineral water the better”. TDS, hardness, pH and residual chlorine change extraction, liquor colour and aroma. For most homes, low-to-moderate TDS, low hardness, no chlorine and near-neutral pH is a stable starting point.

Water is over 99% of a cup, yet it is often ignored. Pure water is not automatically good; mineral water is not automatically suitable. Total dissolved solids (TDS), hardness, pH and residual chlorine each change extraction, liquor colour, aroma and taste. Set the water into a reasonable range first; then adjust temperature and time.

Four measures

Measure What it is Effect on tea Practical direction
TDS Total dissolved solids Too low may under-extract and taste hollow; too high suppresses aroma and extraction Start around 50–150 mg/L
Hardness Calcium + magnesium, as CaCO₃ Binds polyphenols, increases sediment, lowers some catechins Low to moderate, about 50–100 mg/L
pH Acidity / alkalinity Alkaline water accelerates catechin oxidation and darkens liquor; acidic may sharpen taste About 6–7
Residual chlorine Disinfection residue Reacts with polyphenols and aroma compounds; gives a chemical off-flavour As close to 0 as possible

These are starting ranges, not universal standards. Tea type, processing and personal preference move you within them.

TDS: not the purer the better

The 2017 green tea study found higher soluble solids, catechins and L-theanine with low TDS (0.59–13.0 ppm), low conductivity and pH 6.04–6.90. With high TDS (315–338 ppm), high conductivity and pH 7.65–8.05, major catechins fell by about 84–93% and the liquor colour deteriorated.

But too low TDS also has problems: fully deionised water can taste hollow and flatten aroma and taste. Practical guides often recommend 50–150 ppm; below 50 may under-extract, above 150 minerals suppress delicate aroma. Many city tap waters are 150–400 ppm, some above 500, and may need filtration or a different source.

Hardness: a trade-off

Hardness is calcium plus magnesium. Japanese trials show harder water reduces green tea catechins (especially EGC and EGCG), increases white sediment (tea cream) and darkens the liquor; above 200 mg/L hardness, vitamin C almost disappears. Yet the same tasters preferred tea brewed with 225.6 mg/L hard water because astringency and bitterness were lower and sweetness higher.

“Optimal hardness” depends on what you want: for clarity, freshness and high catechins, choose low hardness; for softer, less astringent tea, slightly harder water may please. In a separate magnesium test, 16 mg/L reduced black tea sweetness and umami and increased astringency; 4 mg/L was better.

pH and chlorine

Alkaline pH accelerates catechin oxidation, darkens liquor and reduces freshness; acidic pH can sharpen taste. Near neutral, slightly acidic (about 6–7) is a stable range.

Residual chlorine is the most common tap-water issue. Even 0.2–0.3 mg/L can react with polyphenols and floral volatiles, producing a chemical off-flavour or extra bitterness. Boiling does not remove all chlorine; standing, activated carbon filtration or a low-chlorine source works better.

Temperature: tea first, then water

Once water quality is set, temperature matters:

  • green and white tea: about 75–85°C to avoid cooking the leaf;
  • light oolong: about 90–95°C;
  • roasted oolong and black tea: about 95–100°C;
  • post-fermented tea: boiling water, or simmer when appropriate.

Temperature is not a fixed table. Young, broken or heavily dosed leaf may need cooler water or a shorter time.

Household options

  1. Measure first. A TDS pen and pH paper tell you the starting point; without tools, start low-hardness and chlorine-free.
  2. Filter. Activated carbon removes chlorine and some organics; reverse osmosis removes too much mineral, so remineralise slightly.
  3. Read mineral-water labels. “Natural” does not mean suitable; check TDS, calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate.
  4. Do not brew with distilled water directly. It can taste hollow; add a little mineral water or use low-TDS tap water.
  5. Run an A/B test. Same tea and parameters, different water; record colour, aroma and astringency.
  6. Avoid repeated boiling. Heat fresh water in small batches to limit concentration and off-flavour.

Common misreadings

Claim Problem More defensible reading
“Pure water is cleanest and best for tea” Deionisation may under-extract and taste hollow Low TDS with some minerals is more stable
“The more expensive the mineral water, the better” High TDS/hardness can suppress aroma Read the label, not the price
“Harder water tastes sweeter” Hardness affects astringency, extraction and sediment A trade-off; adjust by tea type
“Boiling removes chlorine” A short boil does not remove all chlorine Stand, filter or change source
“pH does not matter” Alkaline water accelerates oxidation and changes colour Keep close to neutral

Source boundary

This page can establish: TDS, hardness, pH and residual chlorine change extraction, aroma, liquor colour and taste; low TDS, low hardness, near-neutral and chlorine-free is a stable starting point; high TDS and hardness can reduce catechin extraction and increase sediment; hardness has a trade-off in astringency.

This page cannot establish: that one bottled water suits every tea; that TDS/pH numbers fully predict flavour; that a filter always reaches a target. Judge by labels, measurements and trial brews; when in doubt, choose a checkable low-chlorine, low-hardness source.
