Abigail's Blend

United States

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

What it is

“Boston tea” can mean a historical event, a commercial brand or a museum product. Abigail's Blend is the museum product: a signature black-tea blend commissioned by the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room, created by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams.

It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773. It is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand either. It is a modern commemorative blend that uses today's black teas to recall the Chinese black teas of 1773. The museum's own description is “reminiscent of the flavours found in the Chinese black teas thrown overboard … but better”.

The 1773 event

On 16 December 1773, Boston colonists boarded three ships at Griffin's Wharf — Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor — and dumped 340 chests of East India Company tea into Boston Harbor. The museum's official page gives the weight as over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); the Massachusetts Historical Society gives “some 90,000 lb”. These are two rounded accounts of the same event, not two events.

The East India Company's 1774 compensation letter itemises the cargo: Bohea, Singlo, Hyson, Congou and other teas. Bruce Richardson says about 80% of the tea thrown overboard was Chinese black tea, with the rest green tea. That is why Abigail's Blend starts with Chinese black tea rather than an Indian black tea base.

The museum fact sheet calls the Boston Tea Party “the single most important event leading up to the American Revolution”. That is the museum's historical framing. It is not a claim that the tea party was the only cause of the Revolution; the political, tax and colonial conflicts behind the event were broader.

The blend: Yunnan black tea, Assam, Nilgiri and Kenya

Bruce Richardson describes the blend in four layers:

Layer Role
Chinese Yunnan black tea Provides the earthy, woody, full-bodied base that recalls the Chinese black teas of 1773
Assam black tea Adds malt, strength and body
Nilgiri black tea Smooths and rounds the blend
Kenyan black tea Adds a bright, familiar British-style foundation

The official material does not give percentages. “Five teas with an exact recipe” or “40% Yunnan, 30% Assam” would be invented. What can be confirmed is the four origins and the structure: a Chinese black-tea base softened and lifted by modern Indian and African black teas.

The product: 3.5 oz loose tea or 14 pyramid bags

The official shop lists:

  • 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea;
  • 14 pyramid tea bags;
  • official price $17.99;
  • ingredient: handpicked black teas;
  • crafted exclusively for the museum by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas;
  • gluten free.

“Handpicked black teas” is an ingredient description, not proof that every step of the blend is handmade. It does suggest leaf-grade tea rather than dust or fannings. Do not turn it into “all-handmade”.

Flavour and drinking

The official serving advice is broad: hot or iced, with or without milk. That tells you the blend is neither a delicate tea that needs to be drunk plain nor a brute-strength breakfast tea that only works with milk. Plain, the Yunnan base and Assam malt come first; with milk, the Nilgiri and Kenyan brightness keep the cup from going flat.

The official material does not give gram, temperature and time figures. As an editorial starting point, try 3 g tea, 200 ml water, 95°C, 3–4 minutes. For milk, make the tea a little stronger; for iced tea, steep the same ratio, remove the leaf, then chill or pour over ice. These are editorial suggestions, not pack instructions.

History vs product

Three things need to stay separate:

  1. The 1773 event: 340 chests of East India Company tea were dumped into Boston Harbor.
  2. The museum product: Abigail's Blend, a modern blend named for Abigail Adams.
  3. The commercial brand: Boston Harbour Tea and similar names are separate market products, not the museum blend.

Merging them into “a Boston tea brand founded in 1773” turns a historical event, a museum souvenir and a commercial brand into one false story. Abigail's Blend is a commemorative tea. It can recall 1773; it is not from 1773.

Tea in United States

The USA has no traditional commercial tea region; Charleston Tea Garden and Hawaii are exceptions. Iced tea, tea bags, specialty tea and coffee competition define its tea culture.

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.

Assam Tea

Assam is the world’s largest contiguous tea-growing region, producing bold, malty black tea on the Brahmaputra plains; CTC is its everyday volume tea, while orthodox Assam offers a fuller-leaf alternative.
