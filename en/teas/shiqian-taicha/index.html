Shiqian Taicha

China

Shiqian Taicha is a geographical-indication green tea from Shiqian County, Guizhou, and the name covers both the local tea population and the product. It received national GI product protection in 2009, was registered as a GI certification mark in 2010 and as a China Well-Known Trademark in 2013; local material calls it one of Guizhou’s three famous teas. In 2025 the county reported 29,510 tonnes of finished tea, RMB 3.2 billion in combined output value, 436,000 mu of tea gardens and more than 50,000 ancient tea plants.

What the name covers

Shiqian Taicha comes from Shiqian County in Tongren, Guizhou. Official material uses the name for both a tea population and a product: the local sexually propagated tea plants on one side, and the green tea made from them on the other.

That overlap is the first thing to get right. Shiqian Taicha is not one cloned cultivar, not one company brand and not a synonym for every “taicha” in Guizhou. Packs sold under the name can come from different enterprises, grades and harvests. What they share is a protected origin and a processing tradition, not a single fixed tasting profile.

The geographical-indication status has several layers:

Status Date / number Meaning
National GI product protection 2009 Protects the product name and the defined origin
GI certification mark 2010 Licensed to qualifying producers by the mark owner
Agricultural GI AGI01644 Provincial registration, applicant Shiqian County Tea Administration
China Well-Known Trademark 2013 A trademark recognition, not a grade for every product

These are not interchangeable. GI protection applies to tea made inside the defined area under the stated rules; it does not automatically certify every package that uses the words “Shiqian Taicha.” The special mark and the authorised producer still matter at the point of sale.

“Tai” means thick leaves, not white fuzz

The official explanation of the name is thick leaves and long, tender stems, which is why locals also call it “Taizicha.” The “tai” is a plant and stem characteristic, not the white fuzz on a young bud.

The name and the appearance claim need to be kept apart. Turning “tai” into “white fuzz” and then building a “downy, honeyed” style on top of it is a guess from the name. Shiqian Taicha can show bud down, but the name does not mean down. Treating the two as the same turns a population name into a superficial appearance claim.

The local type is described as vigorous buds, thicker leaves and good tenderness retention. It suits curled or semi-curled green tea. It is not a “single bud” style, and it does not build its identity on extreme tenderness; it builds it on leaf thickness, solids and staying power.

Origin: low latitude, high altitude, little sunshine

Shiqian sits in north-eastern Guizhou in a humid subtropical monsoon climate. The official shorthand is low latitude, high altitude and low sunshine, a combination that produces frequent cloud, high humidity and a noticeable day–night temperature swing.

The county also markets itself as a “hometown of hot springs” and a “hometown of longevity.” Those are county-level identities, not a description of how the tea gardens are irrigated. The step from “hometown of hot springs” to “hot-spring irrigation” has no reliable source and confuses geothermal resources with the tea garden's water system. Shiqian Taicha grows on rainfall, cloud and soil, not on spa water.

Chemistry: what the county government publishes

The county government publishes this set:

Measure Official figure
Amino acids about 2.6%
Tea polyphenols about 36%
Caffeine about 4.69%
Potassium 19,700 mg/kg
Water extract about 46%

These figures differ sharply from the commonly repeated “4.5–5.5% amino acids” and “0.1–0.3 mg/kg selenium.” The latter give no testing year, sample count or laboratory, so they cannot be treated as a county-wide standard; the county government's published set is the more reliable county-level reference.

The numbers point to a style: moderate-to-high polyphenols and caffeine, high water extract, a cup with structure and staying power. At about 2.6% amino acids, it is not in the extremely sweet, high-amino-acid green tea category represented by Anji Baicha or Huangjinya. It should not be sold as “the sweetest” green tea.

Processing: hand “three roastings, three rollings” alongside machine production

Traditional Shiqian hand-made maofeng follows three roastings and three rollings. After fixing and spreading, the leaf is rolled until it forms strips and juice reaches the surface; then it is roasted, spread and rolled again, twice more, to drive off moisture, tighten the bud-leaf structure and set the aroma and shape.

Tongren Net's feature quotes Wang Fei, a provincial intangible-cultural-heritage maker, on the pressure rhythm: light–heavy–light, hand never leaving the tea, tea never leaving the wok. Too much pressure crushes the leaf; too little leaves it shapeless. That is a useful explanation of what “three roastings and three rollings” actually controls: heat, pressure and the pause between them.

Handwork is not the whole industry. Shiqian had 185 processing enterprises in 2025, and products range from hand maofeng to large-volume green tea. “All Shiqian Taicha is handmade” is false; “only handmade is authentic” also narrows the industry into a niche.

History: the Tang tribute story needs a qualifier

There are two historical layers, and they should not be merged.

The documentary layer is Lu Yu's Classic of Tea, which says of Yizhou: “often obtained, its flavour excellent.” Yizhou was a Tang administrative region larger than today's Shiqian County; local material later connects Yizhou with the Shiqian area. The accurate sentence is “local literature connects the Yizhou tea of the Classic of Tea with Shiqian,” not “Lu Yu named Shiqian directly.”

The gazetteer layer says tea began in the Tang and was sent as tribute; a Ming gazetteer adds a figure of about 100,000 jin a year. These are local-literature claims, not modern statistics. They can be written as “the gazetteer says,” not as “Shiqian sent 100,000 jin to the court every year in the Tang.”

Claims such as “Shiqian tea surpassing eastern Guizhou in the Ming,” “hot-spring tea gardens” and “Lu Yu naming Shiqian” have no reliable source. The safer wording is “Tang-era tea records and a gazetteer tribute claim,” with the specific output and ranking kept inside the gazetteer qualifier.

Industry: 436,000 mu and 29,510 tonnes

Shiqian's 2025 figures:

Measure 2025 figure
Tea garden area 436,000 mu
Area in production 338,500 mu
Finished tea 29,510 tonnes
Combined output value CNY 3.2 billion
Processing enterprises 185
Households involved 28,700
People involved 116,200
Ancient tea plants more than 50,000

On the brand side, the 2023 regional brand value was CNY 1.618 billion, ranked 87th among Chinese tea-region brands; the county is also “China's Taicha Hometown” and a “National Top 100 Tea County.” Those are regional-brand evaluations, not a quality ranking of individual packs.

The 50,000-plus ancient plants are important germplasm, but “many ancient plants” does not mean “every commercial pack comes from ancient trees.” Most volume comes from production gardens; ancient plants are used more for conservation, single-tree selection and small high-end lots. Buyers should read the specific product, not infer from the county's ancient-tree count.

Brewing: treat it as a green tea

Shiqian Taicha is a green tea, so the brewing logic is similar to other curled green teas:

  • water: 80–85°C, not a hard boil straight onto young buds;
  • leaf: 3 g to 150–200 ml;
  • time: 1–2 minutes for the first steep, then about 30 seconds more each round;
  • vessel: glass or a gaiwan; glass shows the leaf opening.

Its thicker leaves and high water extract usually give more staying power than a fine-bud tea. It still should not be left to stew: a long, sealed steep brings out a cooked, flat note and astringency together. If the cup is bitter, lower the temperature and shorten the time before reaching for sugar.

Where it sits in Chinese green tea

Shiqian Taicha is not a “famous mountain single-tree” tea and not an “ultra-tender bud” tea. Its identity comes from three things: a Guizhou GI origin, the thick-leaf taicha population, and a curled green tea tradition built on three roastings and three rollings.

Set beside Duyun Maojian and Meitan Cuiya, it reads thicker, more durable and more structured. Beside Anji Baicha, it is not the high-amino-acid sweet type. Beside Biluochun, its buds and leaves are coarser, not the tiny curled-bud style. That positioning is more useful than repeating “one of Guizhou's three famous teas.”

Source boundary

Confirmed here: the GI status, the cultivar/product overlap, the meaning of “tai,” the official chemistry, the 2025 industry figures, the ancient-plant count, the three-roasting-three-rolling tradition, and the gazetteer claims about Tang tribute and Ming output.

Not confirmed here: the cultivar composition of each lot, batch-specific amino acid or polyphenol values, selenium content, a county-wide sensory standard, or the authorisation status of every producer. For a specific pack, read the GI mark, the producer and the test information.

China · Zhejiang

Anji Baicha

Anji Baicha is a green tea from Anji, Zhejiang, despite its name. It is made from the Baiye Yihao cultivar during its spring pale-leaf stage, then fixed, shaped and dried as a green tea. The leaf is jade-white with green veins, the liquor pale yellow, and the taste unusually fresh, sweet and low in bitterness. The 2006 standard is still in force; a 2026 revision takes effect on 2027-04-01.

China · Jiangsu

Biluochun

Biluochun is a pan-fired spring green tea from the Dongting hills in Lake Tai, Jiangsu. The leaf is tiny, tightly curled and covered with white down; the tea is famous for a fresh, sweet, slightly fruity cup and for the tea–fruit intercropping tradition of Dongting Mountain.

Bitaco Green Tea

Bitaco is the specialty tea brand of Agrícola Himalaya S.A., grown at Hacienda Himalaya in La Cumbre, Bitaco, Valle del Cauca, Colombia. The estate is about 254 ha at roughly 1,700–2,050 m, with about half conservation forest and half organic tea, and is described by its owner as Colombia's only commercial tea plantation. Its green tea is not Japanese steamed tea: a publicly verifiable Bitaco green, Selva Curly Green, is pan-fired, with a sweet, nutty, mellow profile rather than a marine sencha profile. The old draft's "1998 Japanese steaming line," "Yabukita/Asatsuyu cultivars," "JAS certification" and "Andean gyokuro" claims have no source and have been removed.
