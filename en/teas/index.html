United States

Abigail's Blend

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

United States

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.

Anji Baicha

Anji Baicha is a green tea from Anji, Zhejiang, despite its name. It is made from the Baiye Yihao cultivar during its spring pale-leaf stage, then fixed, shaped and dried as a green tea. Argentine Tereré

Tereré is yerba mate brewed with cold water and ice. It uses the same leaf and often the same bombilla as hot mate, but the lower temperature gives a lighter, sweeter, less bitter drink. In Argentina it is a summer habit, not a separate tea plant.

Vietnam

Bao Loc Oolong Tea

Bao Loc oolong (Trà Ô Long Bảo Lộc) is a tea region and a family of oolong-style teas from Bao Loc in Lam Dong, Vietnam. It is not a single brand and not a synonym for “Vietnamese Tieguanyin”. Bao Loc sits at about 800 m on the Lam Dong plateau, with cool nights, thick morning fog and a large day-night temperature swing; its soil is deeply weathered basalt, rich in minerals and both well drained and moisture-retentive. Local gardens grow local seed tea, Shan tea and introduced oolong cultivars. Bao Loc oolong is exported to Russia, France, the United States, India, Japan, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore, Taiwan, Hong Kong and the Middle East. Bao Loc VN and Kim Điền operate at different scales.

Biluochun

Colombia

Bitaco Colombian Black Tea

“Colombian highland black tea” is neither a protected appellation nor a national grade. The public, traceable object is the Bitaco black-tea line made by Agrícola Himalaya in the Bitaco area of Valle del Cauca, Colombia. Bitaco describes its black teas as organic, single-origin specialty tea with an amber liquor, smooth and subtly sweet, mellow, with raisin and dried-plum notes. The garden sits at about 1,800 metres and coexists with the Bitaco Regional Forest Reserve. The company's earlier commodity brand was Té Hindú; Bitaco was launched in 2016 as a premium organic line. Cultivar, oxidation percentage and rolling method are not published, so this page does not invent them.

Bitaco is the specialty tea brand of Agrícola Himalaya S.A., grown at Hacienda Himalaya in La Cumbre, Bitaco, Valle del Cauca, Colombia. Canada

Canadian Boreal Herbal Tea

Canadian boreal herbal tea is not a fixed national recipe and not Camellia sinensis tea. It is a working label for caffeine-free infusions made from plants of Canada's boreal forest, most commonly Labrador tea (Rhododendron groenlandicum / R. tomentosum) and spring spruce tips (Picea glauca / P. mariana), with balsam fir and pine sometimes added. It has a long Indigenous and settler drinking tradition, but Labrador tea contains ledol and grayanotoxins; excessive or badly prepared infusions carry real risk. It is not a detox tea and not a substitute for medicine.

Canadian Icewine Tea (Icewine-Flavoured Black Tea)

Canadian Icewine Tea is a flavoured black-tea blend sold in Canada, not icewine and not tea grown in a Canadian garden. The base is usually Sri Lankan or other black tea, combined with icewine, natural flavours or grape flavour; formulations vary, and some contain real Ontario icewine while others use flavour only. Ontario Icewine itself is regulated: grapes must be naturally frozen on the vine, picked at -8°C or lower and pressed immediately, with juice at least 35 Brix. Türkiye

Çaykur Altınbaş (Golden Head Black Tea)

Çaykur Altınbaş is a Turkish black-tea product and grade, not a cultivar and not the “golden head” plant part itself. Çaykur's official product copy says the bud at the top and the two small fresh leaves beneath it are the secret of Altınbaş; the blend carries the highest proportion of golden-tipped, curled leaf, a trademark astringency, a high aroma and enough strength that a small amount makes a large brew, with no additives or chemicals. It comes in a 400 g tin and in 200 g and 500 g bags. Çaykur's training notes explain the Altın Tip / Altınbaş look as an orange-gold “golden tip” formed when the buds are heavily hairy and processing is appropriate. It is not a Turkish Jin Junmei, not a single-estate tea and not an ancient-tree product.

Cha DoiChang Oolong Green Tea

Doi Tung Oolong

Doi Tung Oolong is a regional and trade name for oolong tea from the Doi Tung highlands in Chiang Rai, northern Thailand. It is not a current official tea SKU of the DoiTung social enterprise. Gambung Green Tea (Gamboeng Peko Super)

Gambung Green Tea, in the public record, is the PPTK product **Gamboeng Green Tea Peko Super** — not a generic “Gambung village” tea and not a cultivar. Gimhae Janggun-cha (Janggun Tea)

Gimhae Janggun-cha is a local tea from Gimhae, Gyeongsangnam-do, South Korea, not a generic “Gimhae green tea.” It is made from a large, thick-leaf local tea population, primarily as non-fermented green tea but also as an oxidised product. The name is tied to a recorded Goryeo-era story in which King Chungnyeol gave the tea the title “Janggun” (general); an older local tradition traces the plants to Queen Heo Hwang-ok and seeds brought from India in AD 48. Gimhae has promoted the tea since 1999, with wild tea clusters on the slopes of Bunseongsan. Official material describes a tea that holds up to 100°C water, has a wild-rose-like aroma and a sweet umami finish. The old draft's 1992/1995/1998/2005 timeline, 200–300 tonne output, 30% market share, Yabukita and dragon-king legend had no verifiable source and were removed.

China · Guizhou

Guiding Yunwu Tribute Tea

Guiding Yunwu Tribute Tea is a nationally protected regional green tea from Guiding County in Guizhou, China. Its core production area is Yunwu Town, and it is a geographical indication, not a single company brand. It received national agricultural GI recognition in September 2010 and a geographical indication certification mark in July 2013. At the end of 2024 the county had 255,600 mu (about 17,040 ha) of tea gardens, 247,300 mu (about 16,487 ha) in production, and an output of 11,807 tonnes, up about 5.35% year on year; Xinhua's contemporaneous figure for primary-sector output value was about RMB 580 million. Local records describe 2,000-plus years of tea growing, 1,000-plus years of tribute tea and 500-plus years of Buddhist tea history; in 1790 the Qianlong court allocated 420 taels of silver and a “Wangu Liufang” tribute stele was erected. The main cultivar is Guiding Niaowang, approved as a provincial improved variety in June 2014, and DB52/T 547—2017 grades the product as Zhengong, Special Grade, Grade 1 and Grade 2.

South Korea

Hadong Green Tea

Hadong green tea is a geographical-indication green tea region in Hadong County, South Korea, not a single brand. Official material dates the beginning to 828, when Kim Daeryeom returned from Tang China with tea seeds and the king ordered them planted on Jirisan; the county describes about 1,200 years of tea history and calls itself the first tea cultivation site. Hadong was designated a wild tea industrial special district in 2006, a Korean Important Agricultural Heritage (KIAHS) in 2015 and a FAO Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System (GIAHS) in 2017. The 2008 official count was 2,100 tea households, 1,048 ha, 2,230 tonnes and KRW 26.1 billion. Its signature process is *deokkeum*, repeated pan-firing and rubbing, not Japanese steaming; the tea fields run along the Seomjin River and Hwagae Stream, with fog, high humidity and a wide day–night temperature range. Old claims about a “Chinese small-leaf descendant,” “Jirihyang,” wild tea trees “discovered in the 1970s” and a Japanese-colonial break in mechanisation have no source and are removed.

China · Shaanxi

Hanzhong Xianhao

Hanzhong Xianhao is a geographical indication regional public brand for green tea in Hanzhong, Shaanxi, China. It is not a single company, a single cultivar, or a single retail SKU. In 2007, AQSIQ Announcement No. 178 placed Hanzhong Xianhao under geographical indication product protection across eight tea-producing counties, 112 townships and 894 villages. The official notice defines three fresh-leaf grades — Special, First and Second — and a seven-step process: spreading, killing the green, carding, shaping, raising the white hairs, drying and sorting. In 2024, Hanzhong had 132.4 wan mu (about 88,270 ha) of tea gardens, 71,000 tonnes of total tea output and CNY 11.07 billion in dry maocha value; Hanzhong Xianhao itself produced 1,675 tonnes worth CNY 1.98 billion. The 2025 brand value was CNY 5.098 billion, and the national geographical indication protection demonstration zone passed an on-site review in September 2025.

United States

Hawaii Volcano Green Tea

Hawaii volcano green tea is a regional name for green tea grown on the volcanic highlands of Hawaiʻi Island, especially around Volcano at about 4,000 ft and the north-east slope of Mauna Loa at about 3,000 ft. It is not a single brand, and not all Hawaiian tea comes from volcanic highlands. Tea was introduced to Hawaiʻi in 1887. In 1999–2001, USDA/ARS and the University of Hawaiʻi CTAHR established clonal trials at Waiakea (600 ft), Mealani (2,800 ft) and Volcano (4,000 ft); Mealani and Volcano produced excellent green and oolong tea. Big Island Tea, founded in 2001, makes small-batch handmade green and black tea on a volcanic-ash garden at about 3,000 ft on Mauna Loa. Volcano Winery, at about 4,000 ft near the Kilauea summit, took cuttings in 2005, planted in 2006, reached one acre by 2011 and makes black and white tea. Tea Hawaii works in native-forest agroforestry at Volcano Village. Public sources do not give a single “volcanic mineral” sensory standard, so volcanic-ash soil cannot be treated as a guaranteed flavour.

Japan

Hojicha

Hojicha is Japanese green tea roasted after processing; the leaf turns brown, the liquor becomes amber, and the aroma moves from fresh grass to toasted nuts and caramel.
