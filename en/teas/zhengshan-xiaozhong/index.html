Zhengshan Xiaozhong

What it is

Zhengshan Xiaozhong is a Souchong black tea from the Tongmu Pass area of Wuyishan, Fujian. “Zhengshan” is a traditional origin term, usually referring to Tongmu Pass and nearby high-mountain gardens; “Xiaozhong” is associated with the local small-leaf tea and product type. It is often described as one of China’s earliest black teas to reach Europe, but “all the world’s black tea originated from Zhengshan Xiaozhong” is an overgeneralisation; the origin and spread of black tea are still debated.

Origin and cultivar

The traditional core is Tongmu Pass in Wuyishan, near the Wuyi Mountain national nature reserve. Local population cultivars (菜茶) are the traditional material, with relatively small leaves and rich composition. Souchong black tea is also made outside Tongmu, but not all can be called “Zhengshan Xiaozhong”; GI products have specific origin requirements. Check origin, standard and producer on the label.

Processing

Traditional Zhengshan Xiaozhong goes through withering, rolling, fermentation, “passing the red pan” and pine-smoke drying. Pine-smoke drying creates the pine aroma and longan-like sweetness, usually with pine wood or smoke; modern products may reduce or omit smoking. Smoke level, wood type, roasting time and leaf grade change the style. Jinjunmei is a bud-only black tea developed from the Zhengshan Xiaozhong tradition, but it is a different product.

Quality and flavour

Dry leaves are tightly rolled and dark. Traditional smoked tea has a deep red liquor with pine smoke, dried longan and woody notes, and a mellow sweet taste. Unsmoked or lightly smoked products show more floral and sweet notes. Smoke is not “the heavier the better”; char, pungency or smoke that hides the tea usually indicates poor control.

History and boundaries

Zhengshan Xiaozhong entered European trade around the seventeenth century and is often mentioned in early China–Britain tea history. The old story of soldiers sleeping on tea leaf and causing accidental fermentation is local legend, not established origin. The English name Lapsang Souchong also has multiple explanations and should not be reduced to one dialect transliteration. The 2002 GI protection and Wuyi black-tea local standards should be checked against announcements and standard texts.

Common misconceptions

  • Not all Zhengshan Xiaozhong is heavily smoked. Traditional smoked tea is one style; unsmoked and lightly smoked products exist.
  • “Zhengshan” is not all Wuyi black tea. It refers to the traditional core origin; GI products have a specific area.
  • “The ancestor of all black tea” needs qualification. It is an important early black tea, but a slogan is not tea history.
  • Heavy smoke is not necessarily good. Char, pungency and smoke that mask the tea are not virtues.

Buying and keeping

For Zhengshan Xiaozhong, check that the strips are tight and the colour dark lustrous, then smell for pine smoke, flowers, fruit or sweetness. Smoke should be natural rather than sharp; the liquor should be bright red, sweet and smooth rather than harsh. Smoke levels vary widely with processing, and the name “Zhengshan” alone does not prove origin. Store sealed, dark, dry and away from odours, and finish an opened packet reasonably soon. If the aroma fades badly or a mouldy smell appears, do not keep it.

Wuyi Mountains tea area

Wuyi rock tea is a Danxia-landscape oolong from northern Fujian. True rock, half-rock, river tea and gorge microclimates, with shaking, rolling and charcoal roast, produce the “rock bone and flower aroma” of Da Hong Pao, Rou Gui and Shui Xian.

Jinjunmei

Jinjunmei is a single-bud black tea developed around the 2000s in the Tongmu Pass area of Wuyishan. The name is a commercial name, not a cultivar; it is usually lightly oxidised and not heavily smoked, with honey and floral-fruity notes. “All golden buds” and “top grade” are marketing claims, not origin or process proof.

Abigail's Blend

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.
