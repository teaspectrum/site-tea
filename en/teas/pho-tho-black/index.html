A regional tea, not a single product

Phú Thọ black tea (chè đen Phú Thọ) is black tea from Phú Thọ Province in northern Vietnam. “Black tea” here means chè đen, a fully oxidised tea in the same family as what English calls black tea. It is not a Chinese-style dark tea such as pu-erh or liubao, and it is not a post-fermented tea.

It is also not a brand, an estate or a fixed recipe. The province has dozens of processors, a cluster of traditional tea villages and thousands of growers. Products range from Orthodox leaf to CTC granules. The name is best understood as a region plus a tea type: the origin has a boundary, the product does not have one standard template.

That shapes the writing. There is no province-wide sensory table to publish, and no single company's export formula should be presented as the provincial norm. What can be documented is scale, product mix, processing routes and export direction.

Scale: 14,500 hectares, second nationally by area

The provincial government's 2026 figures:

Measure Figure
Tea area about 14,500 ha
Fresh leaf 193,000 tonnes a year
National position second by area
Large processors 76
Traditional tea villages 18
Cooperatives 12
Tea-growing communes 58
Growing-area codes 3,800 ha

Phú Thọ sits in the midland and mountain zone of northern Vietnam, with tea on sloping hills. The scale makes it one of Vietnam's most important tea regions, but not the only one: Thái Nguyên, Sơn La and Lâm Đồng have different product profiles.

Growing-area codes are an export tool. They register blocks, area and growers so buyers can trace residue and origin. The 3,800 ha under codes are not the same as saying all 14,500 ha have reached that level of traceability.

Product mix: about 70% black tea, Orthodox and CTC together

About 70% of Phú Thọ's processed tea is black tea; the rest is mainly green tea. Black tea is export-oriented, with markets in India, China, Pakistan, Indonesia, the UK, Russia and the Middle East, and more recent sales to the US, the EU and Japan.

Two processing routes coexist:

  • Orthodox: whole-leaf or broken-leaf tea, with rolling and drying designed to keep more of the leaf structure; suited to loose-leaf drinking and leaf presentation.
  • CTC: crush–tear–curl, a granular product that extracts fast and strong; used in tea bags and milk-blend formulations.

Asia Tea's factory in Yên Lập is a concrete example: it started with an Orthodox black tea line in 2009, added CTC in 2010, and handles 150 tonnes of fresh leaf a day. That is one company's timeline, not the provincial timeline, but it shows that both routes are real in Phú Thọ.

Neither route is “better.” Orthodox supports origin storytelling and loose-leaf brewing; CTC supports stable, strong, low-cost export blends. A page that calls all Phú Thọ tea “whole-leaf” or all of it “broken” is wrong.

Flavour: no province-wide standard

The official material does not publish a single sensory standard for Phú Thọ black tea. What can be said is that it is fully oxidised, usually red in the cup and relatively strong, suitable for drinking plain or with milk and for blending. Specific notes — malt, caramel, wood, floral — depend on cultivar, season, oxidation and drying.

That is the main difference from a “famous mountain” tea. Phú Thọ's competitive advantage is scale, export standards and cost, not one estate's signature aroma. At the point of sale, grade, sieve size, season and production date matter more than the words “Phú Thọ.”

History: 1958 is the verifiable marker

The old draft pushed the history back to “Ly dynasty tribute to the Song” and added a late-19th-century “first black tea factory in northern Vietnam,” a French botanist's “500-year wild tea trees” and a Hùng King tea-ancestor legend. None of those had a reliable source in this review, and they are removed.

The modern marker that can be documented is 20 July 1958, when Ho Chi Minh visited the Phú Thọ tea factory in Đào Giã. That shows early state attention to the area's tea industry; it does not prove that Vietnamese black tea began in 1958.

Phú Thọ's tea economy took its modern shape through state factories, household growing and export channels in the later 20th century. The processors, traditional villages and cooperatives visible today are the result of that longer evolution. Specific “firsts” without a source are left out.

Exports: about 30,000 tonnes in seven months of 2026

Phú Thọ tea is export-oriented. In the first seven months of 2026, the province exported about 30,000 tonnes, with markets including the Middle East, Russia, China, Pakistan, India, Indonesia and the UK, plus efforts in the US, the EU and Japan.

That structure explains two things:

  1. Why black tea dominates: international demand for CTC and Orthodox black tea is stable, and export blends scale more easily than a single high-end green tea.
  2. Why grade matters more than origin story: export buyers look at sieve size, strength, residues and delivery reliability before they look at a provincial narrative.

This is not to say Phú Thọ has no high-end tea. The province is also promoting the “Trà Đất Tổ” brand and higher-value products. But the mass export base is still black tea, CTC and blends. Keep “export commodity” and “premium niche” separate so the two do not contradict each other.

Brewing: treat it as black tea

Phú Thọ black tea is fully oxidised, so brew it like black tea:

  • leaf: 3 g to 150–200 ml;
  • water: 90–95°C;
  • time: 2–3 minutes for the first steep; 1–2 minutes for CTC;
  • additions: milk, sugar or lemon work; CTC is the better base for milk tea.

Orthodox leaf holds up for two or three steeps. CTC extracts fast and is strong in the first cup; do not leave it sitting. If the cup is too astringent, reduce leaf or shorten the time rather than trying to “wash” the tea.

Where it sits in Vietnamese tea

Phú Thọ's value is not that it is “older than Thái Nguyên” or “more premium than Sơn La.” It is a documented example of Vietnam's bulk tea export capacity: 14,500 ha, 193,000 tonnes of fresh leaf, 76 processors, about 70% black tea and a 30,000-tonne export flow.

For anyone learning Vietnamese tea, Phú Thọ is a useful starting point because it is neither a single estate nor an abstract category. It has a defined region, defined processing routes and defined export markets. Accept that it is a regional tea type first, and the “largest,” “century-old tree” sales language loses its grip.

Source boundary

Confirmed here: the regional fully oxidised identity, 14,500 ha, 193,000 tonnes of fresh leaf, second nationally by area, 76 processors, 18 tea villages, 12 cooperatives, 58 tea communes, 3,800 ha of growing-area codes, about 70% black tea, Orthodox and CTC coexistence, about 30,000 tonnes exported in seven months of 2026, and the 1958 Ho Chi Minh visit.

Abigail's Blend

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.

Assam Tea

Assam is the world’s largest contiguous tea-growing region, producing bold, malty black tea on the Brahmaputra plains; CTC is its everyday volume tea, while orthodox Assam offers a fuller-leaf alternative.
