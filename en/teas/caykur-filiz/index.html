Çaykur Filiz (Bud-Leaf Black Tea)

Türkiye

Çaykur Filiz is a black-tea blend from Türkiye's state tea enterprise, named after the Turkish word for a shoot or bud. The official description is narrow and useful: it contains a high proportion of tea buds, has no additives, and is described as smooth with a lasting aroma. It is not a cultivar, a single estate, or the product of the old draft's ÇT-9 clone, 2008 project, 2011 launch or “feminine afternoon tea” story. The official pack sizes are 500 g, 1,000 g and 2,000 g. Filiz belongs to the everyday blended side of the Çaykur range, while Altınbaş is the golden-tip, more astringent line.

What it is: a black-tea blend named for its bud content

Çaykur Filiz is a black tea from Çaykur, Türkiye's state tea enterprise. The name comes from the Turkish filiz, meaning a shoot, bud or young growth. The official product description is just as direct: it contains a high proportion of tea buds.

That sentence sets three limits:

  • it is a black-tea blend, not a single cultivar and not a single estate;
  • “Filiz” is a product name, not an official Turkish tea grade code;
  • a high bud proportion does not mean “all buds” and does not make it a limited golden-tip product.

The official page is short but specific:

Item Official information
Product Filiz Çayı / Filiz Tea
Type Black tea
Material High proportion of tea buds
Additives None; Çaykur describes it as a natural tea like its other teas
Flavour Smooth, with a lasting aroma
Packs 500 g, 1,000 g, 2,000 g

None of the old draft's “ÇT-9 clone”, “2008 project”, “2011 launch” or “feminine afternoon tea” material survives here. Those claims had no official source. Filiz is a commercial blend built around bud content, not a secret cultivar project.

Why “Filiz” is not a grade

In Turkish, filiz can mean a new shoot, a bud or a plant sprout. On a tea pack it describes the raw material, not a separate cultivar.

Two practical consequences follow.

First, more buds usually means younger material. Buds and the first two leaves differ from older leaves in their polyphenol, amino-acid and fibre balance, which tends to produce a softer, less coarse brew. Çaykur's own “smooth” description fits that material profile.

Second, more buds is not the same as a higher grade. Turkish tea grading and blending also weigh aroma, liquor colour, astringency, origin and processing control. Filiz is positioned as an everyday blend, not as a “buds only” luxury grade.

The product it is most easily confused with is Altınbaş. Altınbaş is another Çaykur line, described around a high proportion of golden tips, a characteristic astringency and the ability to make a strong brew from a small amount of leaf. Filiz is described around bud content and smoothness. Both contain buds; they are different positions in the range, not two names for the same tea.

The corporate context: a blend, not a single garden

Çaykur is Türkiye's state tea enterprise, established in 1983 under Statutory Decree 112 and headquartered in Rize. Its corporate page lists 47 wet-tea factories, one packing factory and 6,600 tonnes/day of wet-tea processing capacity. That is an industrial network across the eastern Black Sea tea belt, not a single estate.

As a Çaykur blend, Filiz draws on that procurement and processing network. The official page does not publish its exact blend ratio, the origin of each lot or the cultivars used. Claims such as “all from the Çamlıhemşin valley” or “single-garden ancient trees” are therefore unsupported.

The trade-off is consistency: a blend aims to keep aroma, strength and astringency predictable across seasons and lots. It will not show the vintage variation of a single-garden spring tea.

Brewing: the Turkish double-pot method

Filiz is loose tea, and the natural vessel is the Turkish double pot, çaydanlık: water boils in the lower pot while tea steeps in the smaller upper pot, heated by steam and hot water.

The Rize Çayı geographical-indication page gives the traditional proportions: about one teaspoon of tea per glass, boiling water poured over the leaf in the upper pot, and 15–20 minutes of steeping, then dilution with hot water from the lower pot. That is the general Turkish method, not a Filiz-specific official recipe.

A workable starting point:

Item Suggestion
Leaf About 1 teaspoon per glass, scaled to the upper pot
Water Boiling
Steep 15–20 minutes to make a concentrated dem
Serving Pour the dem, then dilute with hot water to açık (light) or koyu (strong)
Glass Ince belli, the thin-waisted Turkish tea glass

Filiz's higher bud share can turn bitter if the leaf stays in the pot too long. Start at 15 minutes; if the brew is too strong, shorten the upper-pot steep or use less leaf.

What the official page does not say

Common claim What is actually confirmed
“ÇT-9 clone” No official cultivar code; the old draft invented it
“Project started in 2008, launched in 2011” No official launch date; do not present it as history
“Designed for women's afternoon tea” No gender positioning in any official source
“Single Çamlıhemşin valley” The official page does not identify a single origin
“Limited golden-bud Filiz” The official claim is a high bud proportion, not an all-bud limited edition
“Rose-aroma legend” No official or reliable source; removed

The defensible version is: a Çaykur black-tea blend with a high proportion of buds, no additives, a smooth profile and 500/1,000/2,000 g packs. That is more useful than a fabricated cultivar story.

Where it sits in the Çaykur range

Çaykur's range runs from everyday Rize Turist and Altınbaş to organic tea, green tea and gift packs. Filiz sits on the softer, bud-forward side of the everyday blends: higher bud content, official “smooth” language and a lasting aroma.

If you are used to the strong tea served in Turkish streets, Filiz may feel softer; if standard Rize tea feels too astringent, it is a reasonable place to start. Tasting it beside Altınbaş makes the range legible: one line is built around golden tips and astringency, the other around bud content and smoothness.

Abigail's Blend

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.

Assam Tea

Assam is the world’s largest contiguous tea-growing region, producing bold, malty black tea on the Brahmaputra plains; CTC is its everyday volume tea, while orthodox Assam offers a fuller-leaf alternative.
