Assam Tea

India · 阿萨姆红茶

Assam is the world’s largest contiguous tea-growing region, producing bold, malty black tea on the Brahmaputra plains; CTC is its everyday volume tea, while orthodox Assam offers a fuller-leaf alternative.

One-line identity

Assam is the benchmark for “strong black tea”. It does not win on floral aroma; it wins on body, malt sweetness and the ability to stand up to milk. The useful way to understand Assam is through its river-valley terroir, its large-leaf plant material and the two processing routes, orthodox and CTC.

Place and terroir

Assam sits in India’s northeast, with the Brahmaputra running west across the state. The Tea Board of India gives garden elevations of only 45–60 m and annual rainfall of 250–380 cm: a hot, humid tropical valley. River alluvium builds deep, fertile, well-drained soil, and tea grows fast. The result is fat buds, high polyphenol levels and a liquor with obvious strength.

That is the opposite of Darjeeling’s cool high-altitude delicacy, and different again from Nilgiri’s floral highland style. Assam is not a “fine” region in the porcelain-cup sense. Its value is power and compatibility with milk, spice and sugar.

The Tea Board describes Assam as the world’s largest contiguous tea-growing area. That is a statement about scale, not a guarantee that every garden is equal. Within the valley there are real differences: low riverside gardens tend to give volume and strength; slightly raised, better-drained gardens give cleaner aroma. A named garden and invoice number tell you more than the word “Assam”.

Plant and two processing routes

Assam’s main material is the large-leaf Assam type, Camellia sinensis var. assamica: bigger leaves, fatter buds, higher polyphenols. After oxidation it gives a deep red liquor and a strong, malty cup. The Tea Board notes that the Tocklai Experimental Station carries out clonal propagation and continuous research, which is why much of today’s Assam is selected planting material rather than wild seed.

Assam makes two kinds of tea:

  • CTC (crush–tear–curl). Withered leaf passes through toothed rollers that crush, tear and curl it into pellets, then oxidises and dries. It extracts fast, gives high colour and strength, and is the base of most tea bags and breakfast blends.
  • Orthodox. Withered, rolled, oxidised and dried while keeping the leaf strip. Aroma is clearer, malt sweetness finer, and it can be brewed as a single-origin tea. Assam orthodox is a registered GI.

“CTC is cheap tea” is too simple. CTC is a process aimed at fast, consistent, strong extraction. It can be excellent everyday tea or cheap dust made from coarse leaf. Raw material, oxidation control and drying decide quality, not whether the leaf was broken.

Flavour and brewing

Dry leaf is usually dark brown to black: tight pellets for CTC, wiry strips for orthodox, with golden tips on better orthodox grades. The liquor is deep amber to reddish-brown. Aroma is malt, toast, ripe fruit and a little earthy warmth. The entry is strong, tannin is firm, and the finish is sweet, often with caramel or nut notes.

Brewing suggestions:

  • CTC. Boiling water, 3–5 g per 200 ml, 3–5 minutes. For milk tea, brew strong enough that the milk does not erase the tea.
  • Orthodox. 90–95°C, 3–4 g per 200 ml, about 3 minutes. Good first- or second-flush leaf can go 2–3 minutes to keep malt sweetness ahead of bitterness.
  • Cold brew. CTC extracts slowly in cold water; rinse with hot water first or extend the cold-steep time.

Do not judge Assam by “high aroma”. Judge body, malt sweetness and structure. A thin cup, or one that tastes only bitter with no sweetness, or one that disappears under milk, points to raw material or blending problems.

Grades and buying

CTC grades are usually BOP, BP, PF and PD, sorted by particle size. Finer particles extract faster and give more strength but usually less aroma and layering. Orthodox grades include GFOP, TGFOP and FTGFOP1; more tips and a more complete strip generally mean a higher grade.

When buying, check:

  1. Process. CTC and orthodox are different drinks, not quality levels.
  2. Flush. Assam orthodox second flush is often praised for balancing aroma and strength; monsoon tea is strong but coarser.
  3. Garden. A single-garden invoice is more informative than a generic “Assam”.
  4. Freshness. Black tea oxidises and loses aroma. Keep it sealed, dark and cool, and finish within about a year.

History and industry

Assam’s commercial tea industry expanded in the nineteenth century with colonial trade, but the old page’s “Robert Bruce discovered wild tea in 1823” and “the first Assam lot caused a sensation in London in 1839” are not supported by the official sources used here. What is solid: Assam is today one of India’s most important black-tea regions, the Tocklai research station has operated since 1911, and CTC became the volume process for mainstream Assam in the mid-twentieth century.

Tea in India

India is a major tea producer. Assam, West Bengal, Nilgiri and Darjeeling support both CTC and orthodox systems, shaped by auctions and domestic milk-tea demand.

Abigail's Blend

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.
