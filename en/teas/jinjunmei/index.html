What it is

Jinjunmei is a single-bud black tea developed around the 2000s in the Tongmu Pass area of Wuyishan. It is a commercial name, not a tea cultivar or a separate national product category. It usually uses single buds from local population cultivars, is relatively lightly oxidised, and is mostly not heavily smoked; the aroma tends toward honey, floral-fruity and sweet notes. Calling it “the highest grade of black tea” is marketing language, not a standard definition.

Origin and material

The core origin is often identified as Tongmu Pass, but “Jinjunmei” as a commercial name does not have a unified GI definition like Anxi Tieguanyin. Producers may use single buds from Tongmu or nearby gardens, and may also use material from other areas. Whether the material is truly single-bud, from a high-mountain garden, and processed on the day of picking is more informative than the name on the package.

Processing

Jinjunmei uses black-tea processing, usually withering, rolling, fermentation, drying and refining. Compared with Zhengshan Xiaozhong, it emphasises single-bud material and lighter fermentation, and most products are not heavily pine-smoked, preserving honey and floral-fruity notes. Withering time, fermentation degree and drying temperature vary by producer; “all hand-made” and “three days to finish” are not universal standards.

Quality and flavour

Dry tea is fine and slender, with golden tips and dark brown buds. Liquor is golden to orange-yellow, with honey, floral-fruity and sweet aromas, a sweet, soft taste and a smooth body. The “golden” colour comes from bud colour and processing, not “the more golden the better”; products with uniformly golden tips should prompt questions about material and processing. Price reflects material, origin, brand and market, not a quality grade by itself.

Market and boundaries

Jinjunmei was introduced to the market around 2005 by Wuyishan tea makers and helped drive the single-bud and high-price black-tea market. Old records about its creation, bud counts, auction prices and “ceiling of black tea” are promotional and need checking against company records, local tea-industry materials and testing reports. It is not a GI product name; check origin, material, standard and producer rather than the name and gold packaging.

Common misconceptions

  • Jinjunmei is not a cultivar. It is a commercial name; material may come from different cultivars.
  • Jinjunmei is not Zhengshan Xiaozhong. Processing and material emphasis differ; it is usually lighter-fermented and less smoked.
  • Golden colour is not quality. Colour, down and price can be amplified by processing or packaging; aroma, taste and cleanliness matter more.
  • “Tongmu Pass” needs traceability. Without origin, production records and testing, the name alone proves little.

Wuyi Mountains tea area

Wuyi rock tea is a Danxia-landscape oolong from northern Fujian. True rock, half-rock, river tea and gorge microclimates, with shaking, rolling and charcoal roast, produce the “rock bone and flower aroma” of Da Hong Pao, Rou Gui and Shui Xian.

Zhengshan Xiaozhong

Zhengshan Xiaozhong is a small-leaf black tea from Tongmu Pass, Wuyishan, Fujian, traditionally pine-smoked and known for pine smoke and longan sweetness. It is one of China’s earliest black teas to reach Europe, but “all black tea originated here” is an overstatement.

Abigail's Blend

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.
