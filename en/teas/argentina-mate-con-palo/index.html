Yerba Mate with Stems

Argentina

“Con palo” is stemmed yerba mate: dried leaf blended with a controlled share of young stems. The stems soften the cup, add a woody sweetness and keep the bombilla drawing evenly. Argentina’s geographical indication caps stems at 35% and requires at least 65% leaf.

In one sentence

“Con palo” means “with stems”. Argentine and Paraguayan yerba is traditionally a blend of dried leaf and young stem, not pure leaf. Stems make the drink rounder, sweeter and less aggressive, and they keep the bombilla flowing. “Sin palo” — stem-free — is the denser, more bitter alternative.

The 65/35 rule

INYM’s “Yerba Mate Argentina” geographical indication gives the stemmed category a precise boundary:

  • leaf (dried, broken or powdered): at least 65%;
  • stems, fragments and fibre (coarse or fine): no more than 35%.

This is not a licence to add “anything woody”. It is a defined product class with a floor and a ceiling. Brands vary within that range. More stem usually means a softer, sweeter cup; less stem means a more concentrated, more bitter one.

Why stems belong there

Stems are not waste. They do real work:

  • Different solubility: leaf carries most of the bitterness and body; stems add a gentler sweetness and a woody aroma.
  • Better flow: stems keep the leaf bed open, so water passes evenly and the bombilla clogs less.
  • Lower bite: stemmed mate usually feels less intense than pure leaf, which makes it easier to drink in quantity.
  • Cost: the blend is traditional and makes everyday mate more affordable.

What stems do not tell you

The word “con palo” describes a blend category, not a quality grade. A stemmed product can be made from good leaf or poor leaf, from a single estate or from a blend of lots, and it can be lightly or heavily smoked. The label does not tell you the leaf grade, the harvest date, the curing temperature or the age of the pack.

That is why two bags with the same “con palo” wording can taste nothing alike. One may be sweet, grassy and clean; another may be dusty, sour and dominated by smoke. Treat the category as a starting point for choosing a style, then judge the actual leaf with your nose and mouth.

Stems also do not make a tea “light” in caffeine terms. They change the balance of flavour and the draw, but the leaf still supplies most of the caffeine. If you are sensitive, watch how much dry leaf you load, not just the stem percentage.

Flavour

  • Dry leaf: dark green broken leaf with pale yellow-brown stems; grassy, hay, light smoke.
  • Liquor: yellow-green to amber, usually a shade lighter than stem-free.
  • Aroma: grass, wood and gentle sweetness; smoke is typically lower than in pure leaf.
  • Taste: round entry, mild bitterness, quick sweetness; low aggression.
  • Body: slightly thinner than pure leaf, but smoother and easier.

Brewing

Stemmed mate works in all three traditional forms:

  • Hot mate: 70–95°C, 25–50 g, gourd and bombilla; the stems help the draw.
  • Tereré: 5–8°C water and ice; a coarse stemmed cut is especially resistant to clogging.
  • Mate cocido: boiling water off the heat, 3–5 minutes, then strain; stems make the result softer and sweeter.

Stemmed vs stem-free

Dimension Con palo Sin palo
Blend Leaf ≥65%, stems ≤35% Almost all leaf
Flavour Rounder, sweeter, lower bite Denser, more bitter, more herbal
Body Smooth, slightly thinner Thicker, more astringent
Best for Daily drinking, long sessions, tereré Drinkers who want intensity
Market Common in Argentina and Paraguay More common in Uruguay

Buying checklist

  1. Category: if the pack says “con palo”, it should fit the GI or the brand’s stated blend.
  2. Origin: Misiones or Corrientes.
  3. Cut: medium-fine for hot mate; coarse for tereré.
  4. Aroma: clean grass and light smoke; no mould, acid or sharp tar.
  5. Taste: stemmed does not mean weak or cheap. A good blend is sweet, clean and long-finishing.

Sources and status

The entry relies on INYM’s geographical-indication release for the 65/35 ratio and on INYM’s technical document for the three brewing methods. Old claims that stems are waste filler, that stemmed mate always has less caffeine, or that it is always sweeter have been softened to “usually” and “can”. The page is reviewed for structure and sourcing, not as a product standard.

Argentina

Yerba Mate

Yerba mate is an infusion of Ilex paraguariensis, a South American holly, not a Camellia sinensis tea. Argentina calls it the national infusion and grows most of it in Misiones and Corrientes. The same leaf is drunk hot from a gourd, cold as tereré, or boiled and strained as mate cocido.

Argentina

Organic Argentine Yerba Mate

Organic yerba mate is the same Ilex paraguariensis leaf grown, processed and packed under Argentina’s organic law and certified by an authorised third party. The difference is the production system — soil management, banned synthetic inputs, buffer zones and traceability — not a guarantee of a better-tasting cup.

Argentina

Argentine Tereré

Tereré is yerba mate brewed with cold water and ice. It uses the same leaf and often the same bombilla as hot mate, but the lower temperature gives a lighter, sweeter, less bitter drink. In Argentina it is a summer habit, not a separate tea plant.

Canada

Canadian Boreal Herbal Tea

Canadian boreal herbal tea is not a fixed national recipe and not Camellia sinensis tea. It is a working label for caffeine-free infusions made from plants of Canada's boreal forest, most commonly Labrador tea (Rhododendron groenlandicum / R. tomentosum) and spring spruce tips (Picea glauca / P. mariana), with balsam fir and pine sometimes added. It has a long Indigenous and settler drinking tradition, but Labrador tea contains ledol and grayanotoxins; excessive or badly prepared infusions carry real risk. It is not a detox tea and not a substitute for medicine.

Thailand

Doi Pui Herbal Tea

Doi Pui is a Hmong village inside Doi Suthep-Pui National Park near Chiang Mai, not a verified green-tea growing area. The traceable “Cha Doi Pui” product is an herbal blend built on jiaogulan, mulberry, stevia, ginseng, safflower and ginkgo — a botanical infusion, not tea from Camellia sinensis. The old Chinese draft invented a university tea plantation; that claim has been removed.
