Jinshan Green Tea (Jinshan Shiyu)

China

Jinshan green tea usually refers to Jinshan Shiyu, a historical green tea from Jixi county in Anhui, China. Its core village sits at about 500–900 m, and the tea is picked around Guyu, when the tightly rolled strips are said to resemble rain threads. It received national geographical indication product recognition in 2025. The old machine draft placed it in a Chinese village in Pai, Thailand, and invented a “Veteran Duan” origin story; that claim has no reliable source and has been corrected.

First, the correction

“Jinshan green tea” is a name used by more than one Chinese product. The traceable tea behind this entry is Jinshan Shiyu, a historical green tea from Jinshan village, Shangzhuang township, Jixi county, in Anhui’s southern highlands. The core garden sits at roughly 500–900 metres.

The old machine draft placed this tea in a “Jinshan village” near Pai in Thailand and invented a story about a Chinese veteran, “Duan,” bringing tea from Fujian. No public record supports that. Jixi County Government, the national GI announcement and the county’s spring-tea advisory all place Jinshan Shiyu in Jixi, Anhui. This page follows the traceable record and drops the Thai setting.

The name

According to Jixi’s local tea history, the area has produced green tea since the Tang dynasty, though that claim is local lore rather than an independently dated record. In the Daoguang era of the Qing, a tea called “Jinshan Mingwu” was created; in 1868 it was renamed Jinshan Shiyu.

The name works on two levels:

  • Jinshan — Jinshan village and its high-mountain mist.
  • Shiyu — “seasonal rain,” referring to picking around Guyu, the grain-rain solar term in mid-April; the tightly rolled strips are also said to look like threads of rain.

Local accounts add tribute tea and a 1915 Panama-Pacific gold medal. Those are cultural history, not independently verified facts. What is solid: Jinshan Shiyu is a long-established Jixi green tea, modernized and branded in the twentieth century, and recognized as a national GI product in 2025.

The garden

Jixi lies in southern Anhui, in the same broad tea landscape as Huangshan, Shexian and Jingde. The core Jinshan village gardens are around 500–900 metres, with mist, cool nights and slow growth. The local “Jinshan cultivar” is a place-based population, not a nationally registered clonal cultivar. It is adapted to the area and gives thick buds with a high content of soluble solids.

The photo used here shows tea rows below Jingting Mountain in Xuancheng, also in Anhui. It is a regional image, not a photograph of Jinshan village. It is used to show the terraced tea landscape of southern Anhui, not to claim a specific garden.

Processing

The traditional core is three steps:

  1. Fixing (shāqīng): high heat stops oxidation and preserves the green, fresh character.
  2. Rolling: tightens the strip and puts sap on the leaf surface.
  3. Drying: staged drying fixes the shape and aroma.

Local tea makers describe a “three firings and three rollings” rhythm, with repeated pan-firing and rolling to build the tight, downy strip. Modern production mixes hand work with small machines, but the critical points remain the same: enough heat to stop oxidation, enough pressure to shape without crushing, and a drying curve that preserves freshness.

Flavor

The county’s official description is precise: fat buds, visible white hairs, bright green liquor and a fresh, sweet aftertaste.

This is not a chestnut-heavy Longjing, nor a sharp orchid-scented Dan Cong. Jinshan Shiyu is closer to a clean, soft, slightly toasty green tea: a light chestnut or fresh soybean note, a soft body, and a slow sweetness in the throat. Top lots have clarity and a lingering finish; poor lots taste flat, grassy or astringent.

Brewing

The official advisory gives a practical baseline:

  • Water: 85–90°C, not a rolling boil.
  • Leaf: about 3 g to 150 ml.
  • First steep: about 10 seconds, then lengthen.
  • Re-steep: five to six times.

If the leaf is broken, shorten the first steep. If the strip is whole and tight, give it a few more seconds. Do not leave it sitting in hot water; the buds and white hairs turn bitter quickly.

Buying

  1. Look for the GI mark and the Jinshan Shiyu collective trademark. Ask for the GI-use authorization or a test report if needed.
  2. Check the picking date. Mingqian is scarce and expensive; Yuqian is often better value. Do not chase “first pick” or “special supply” labels.
  3. Check the leaf. Tight strips, visible white hairs and a fresh green color are positives. Broken leaf, dull yellow-brown color and a flat smell suggest stale or rough tea.
  4. Smell the dry leaf. Fresh tea has a clear, persistent aroma; old tea smells flat or closed.
  5. Ignore the mystique. The county’s own advisory warns against “wild ancient tree” and “master-made” marketing. Jinshan Shiyu is a fine green tea, not an ancient-tree pu’er.

Common misreadings

“Jinshan green tea is Thai.”
The old draft’s Pai setting has no reliable source. The core origin is Jinshan village, Jixi, Anhui.

“Jinshan Shiyu is a single clonal cultivar.”
“Jinshan cultivar” is a local population name, not a national registered clone.

“Greener or earlier is always better.”
Color and picking date are only two variables. Fixing, rolling, drying and storage decide the cup.

“All teas called Jinshan green tea are the same.”
The name is used in several places. This page is about the Jixi Jinshan Shiyu. Check origin, trademark and standard.

One line

Jinshan green tea is Anhui Jixi’s Jinshan Shiyu: a rain-thread green tea from a 500–900 m village garden, named for Guyu, and recognized as a national GI product in 2025. It has a real local history; it does not need a Thai plantation story.

China · Zhejiang

Anji Baicha

Anji Baicha is a green tea from Anji, Zhejiang, despite its name. It is made from the Baiye Yihao cultivar during its spring pale-leaf stage, then fixed, shaped and dried as a green tea. The leaf is jade-white with green veins, the liquor pale yellow, and the taste unusually fresh, sweet and low in bitterness. The 2006 standard is still in force; a 2026 revision takes effect on 2027-04-01.

China · Jiangsu

Biluochun

Biluochun is a pan-fired spring green tea from the Dongting hills in Lake Tai, Jiangsu. The leaf is tiny, tightly curled and covered with white down; the tea is famous for a fresh, sweet, slightly fruity cup and for the tea–fruit intercropping tradition of Dongting Mountain.

Colombia

Bitaco Green Tea

Bitaco is the specialty tea brand of Agrícola Himalaya S.A., grown at Hacienda Himalaya in La Cumbre, Bitaco, Valle del Cauca, Colombia. The estate is about 254 ha at roughly 1,700–2,050 m, with about half conservation forest and half organic tea, and is described by its owner as Colombia's only commercial tea plantation. Its green tea is not Japanese steamed tea: a publicly verifiable Bitaco green, Selva Curly Green, is pan-fired, with a sweet, nutty, mellow profile rather than a marine sencha profile. The old draft's "1998 Japanese steaming line," "Yabukita/Asatsuyu cultivars," "JAS certification" and "Andean gyokuro" claims have no source and have been removed.
