Bitaco Green Tea

Colombia

Bitaco is the specialty tea brand of Agrícola Himalaya S.A., grown at Hacienda Himalaya in La Cumbre, Bitaco, Valle del Cauca, Colombia. The estate is about 254 ha at roughly 1,700–2,050 m, with about half conservation forest and half organic tea, and is described by its owner as Colombia's only commercial tea plantation. Its green tea is not Japanese steamed tea: a publicly verifiable Bitaco green, Selva Curly Green, is pan-fired, with a sweet, nutty, mellow profile rather than a marine sencha profile. The old draft's "1998 Japanese steaming line," "Yabukita/Asatsuyu cultivars," "JAS certification" and "Andean gyokuro" claims have no source and have been removed.

What Bitaco is: a brand and an estate, not a tea type

Bitaco is first a place, and then the specialty tea brand Bitaco Unique Colombian Tea, owned by Agrícola Himalaya S.A. The garden is Hacienda Himalaya in the Bitaco area of La Cumbre, Valle del Cauca, Colombia. The company describes it as Colombia's only commercial tea plantation.

So "Bitaco green tea" is not a process type like Longjing or sencha. It is a green-tea line from one estate. Individual products may be pan-fired, roasted or blended; the brand name alone does not tell you which. Equating Bitaco with "Colombian Japanese steamed tea" is inaccurate.

Origin: the western Andes and the edge of the Chocó cloud forest

Hacienda Himalaya sits on the western slope of the Andes' Western Cordillera, on the Pacific-facing side, in La Cumbre. It is in the San Antonio cloud-forest area, next to the Bitaco Regional Forest Reserve, within the Chocó biogeographic region. The company gives the estate as 254 ha at 1,700–2,050 m, with 48–49% conservation forest and 51% organic tea.

The name "Himalaya" can mislead. It is the estate name, not the origin. The Hindú brand has operated in Colombia since 1960 and takes its name from Hacienda Himalaya, which borrows the Himalayan image. The tea itself grows in the Colombian Andes, not in Nepal, India or China.

This is not a tropical lowland. It is an equatorial high-altitude cloud forest: sun in the morning, cloud in the afternoon, high humidity and a slow growing rhythm. The company frames the estate as tea inside a cloud forest and connects it to a biological corridor, water protection and carbon management. That is origin storytelling, not a health claim.

History: Hindú from 1960, Bitaco from 2016

Agrícola Himalaya has made tea under the Hindú brand since 1960, initially for the mass market, tea bags and blends. In 2016 it launched Bitaco Unique Colombian Tea as "Colombia's first national-origin tea," positioned as specialty loose-leaf for export to the US, Canada, Europe and Latin America.

This is not the story of a "1998 Japanese steaming line." The old draft said Bitaco imported Japanese steaming equipment in 1998, planted Yabukita and Asatsuyu, obtained JAS certification and called its tea "Andean gyokuro." None of that appears in Agrícola Himalaya, Bitaco, COP16, Curious Tea or the Colombian public release. What is verifiable is: Hindú from 1960, the Bitaco specialty line from 2016, a single estate, organic and rainforest certification, and a green-tea line whose public example is pan-fired rather than steamed.

Processing: pan-fired/roasted green, not Japanese steamed

The official green-tea language is smooth, gentle, mellow and sweet-nutty, with a clear lemon/jade liquor. That reads more like a pan-fired or roasted green tea than a Japanese sencha, which tends to be marine, savoury and deep-green.

Curious Tea's Selva Curly Green is more specific: curled leaf, pan-fired, picked in December 2025, with fruit, roasted nuts, vegetal notes, a creamy texture and light umami. It is not steamed. The Colombian Emerald Green sold by Simpson & Vail is also described as roasted, with a warm, low-astringency, tropical-sweet profile.

So: at least one publicly verifiable Bitaco green tea is pan-fired/roasted, not Japanese steamed. The company does not say that every green product uses the same process. Buy by product name and lot, not by a generic "Bitaco green" assumption.

The white line uses the softest buds and is described as complex and balanced. The oolong line includes a semi-oxidised whole-leaf product, Dark Andes. The black line is smooth, low-astringency and raisin/dried-plum sweet. They share one estate, but not one process.

Flavour: sweet, low-astringency, nuts and tropical fruit

Bitaco green tea is not built around "seaweed and fresh beans." Its core is sweetness, softness, nuts and low astringency. Selva Curly Green opens with fruit, moves into roasted nuts and a vegetal middle, and finishes with light umami and a creamy texture. Some drinkers read it as a lightly roasted Chinese green rather than a Japanese sencha.

The black teas are smooth, subtly sweet, raisin, dried plum and cocoa, not a strong, malty breakfast tea. The whites are bud-led, floral and clean; the oolong has semi-oxidised fruit, cocoa and raisin sweetness. The house style is "low astringency, easy, clearly sweet," which fits the high-altitude cloud-forest story.

That does not make the tea bland. Its character is tropical highland sweetness, not an imitation of a famous Asian tea. Try a sample before buying a large bag; the lines differ more than the brand name suggests.

Brewing

  • Green: 2.5 g per 250 ml, 80°C, 2–3 minutes; 2 refills. Curious Tea's Selva Curly Green recommendation is exactly 80°C for 2–3 minutes.
  • Black: 3 g per 150 ml, 90–95°C, 3 minutes; 2 refills.
  • White: 3 g per 150 ml, 85°C, 3–4 minutes.
  • Oolong: 3 g per 150 ml, 90°C, 2–3 minutes; 3 refills.
  • Cold brew: 5 g per 500 ml cold water, fridge for 4–6 hours; do not use hot water and call it cold brew.

Bitaco green tea is low in astringency, but it still punishes boiling water. Start at 80°C. The hotter and longer the steep, the more the nutty sweetness gives way to green bitterness.

Buying

  1. Buy the brand and estate. Look for Bitaco / Agrícola Himalaya / Hacienda Himalaya, not only "Colombian tea."
  2. Buy a specific product. Selva Curly Green, Colombian Emerald Green and Organic Premium Green Tea are different products and may use different processes.
  3. Check harvest and lot. Curious Tea's Selva Curly Green states a December 2025 pick; freshness matters for green tea.
  4. Check certification. Ecocert, USDA/EU Organic, Rainforest Alliance and ISO 22000 may appear in different markets. Ask for certificate details when a lot makes a claim.
  5. Do not read "Himalaya" as origin. It is the estate name. The tea comes from the Colombian Andes, not the Himalayas.
  6. Start with samples. The range is wide: green, white, oolong and black differ substantially.

Misconceptions

"Colombia has no tea."
It has a commercial tea estate, but the scale is small. Bitaco / Agrícola Himalaya is the main example, not a mass origin like India, China or Kenya.

"Bitaco is a tea type."
Bitaco is a brand and estate. The type depends on the product: green, white, oolong, black or blend.

"Bitaco green tea is Japanese sencha."
The publicly verifiable Selva Curly Green is pan-fired; the official flavour language is sweet-nutty and mellow, not marine sencha. The old "Yabukita/Asatsuyu/JAS/Andean gyokuro" claims have no source.

"Himalaya means the tea comes from the Himalayas."
Himalaya is the estate name, Hacienda Himalaya. The tea grows in the Colombian Andes.

"Organic certification means absolute safety."
Organic standards regulate inputs and buffer zones. Certification, lot and testing still matter. Do not rely on the word "organic" alone.

"High altitude automatically means better tea."
Altitude is a terroir input; plucking, fixing, rolling, drying and storage decide the cup. Bitaco's value is consistency and sweetness, not an altitude number.

Bottom line

Bitaco is a single-estate specialty brand in the Colombian Andes, at Hacienda Himalaya: 254 ha, about 1,700–2,050 m, half conservation forest and half organic tea. Its green tea is a sweet, nutty, low-astringency, pan-fired/roasted line, not Japanese steamed tea. Buy the product and lot, not the "Andean gyokuro" story.

Tea in Colombia

Colombia's commercial tea is essentially one origin: Bitaco in Valle del Cauca. Planting began in 1946, the Hindú brand launched in 1960, and the Bitaco single-estate specialty line arrived in 2016; one estate carries the national tea industry.

China · Zhejiang

Anji Baicha

Anji Baicha is a green tea from Anji, Zhejiang, despite its name. It is made from the Baiye Yihao cultivar during its spring pale-leaf stage, then fixed, shaped and dried as a green tea. The leaf is jade-white with green veins, the liquor pale yellow, and the taste unusually fresh, sweet and low in bitterness. The 2006 standard is still in force; a 2026 revision takes effect on 2027-04-01.

China · Jiangsu

Biluochun

Biluochun is a pan-fired spring green tea from the Dongting hills in Lake Tai, Jiangsu. The leaf is tiny, tightly curled and covered with white down; the tea is famous for a fresh, sweet, slightly fruity cup and for the tea–fruit intercropping tradition of Dongting Mountain.
