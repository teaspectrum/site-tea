Çaykur Karadeniz Çayı (Bergamot Flavoured)

Türkiye

Çaykur Karadeniz Çayı is a bergamot-flavoured black tea from Çaykur, Turkey's state-owned tea enterprise. This page covers the official “Bergamot Aromalı Karadeniz Çayı 1000gr” SKU. The product page lists 100% Turkish tea, 100% natural, a soft character and a bulk-use positioning; one box holds 10 units, each with a gross weight of 1.25 kg, at a listed price of TRY 398.50, out of stock at the time of review. Karadeniz means “Black Sea” and is a brand/product name, not a declared blend of Rize, Trabzon, Artvin, Giresun and Ordu. Bergamot is an added flavour, not a tea cultivar. Old claims about a five-province blend, a 2010 “Black Sea corridor” project, a 2013 launch and “Karadeniz Koyu/Bahar” versions have no official basis and are removed.

A specific bergamot-flavoured SKU

Çaykur Karadeniz Çayı is not a generic name for “Black Sea tea” and not a tea region. It is a product name: Çaykur's Karadeniz line, in this case the official Bergamot Aromalı Karadeniz Çayı 1000gr, a 1 kg pack with bergamot flavour.

“Karadeniz” means “Black Sea” in Turkish. As a product name it points to the Black Sea tea zone, but it does not mean the official page publishes a five-province blend of Rize, Trabzon, Artvin, Giresun and Ordu. The sales page does not list a blend composition or a fixed five-origin formula. The five-province blend in the old draft was added later.

“Bergamot Aromalı” is bergamot flavour, not a tea cultivar and not bergamot peel mixed into the leaf. This is a flavoured tea, and it should be kept separate from the pure-tea line.

The Çaykur sales page is specific:

“100% Turkish tea” is an origin claim: the leaf comes from Turkey, not from imported tea blended with imported tea. “100% natural” is an official label phrase, not proof of independent organic certification. The two should not be merged.

“1.25 kg” is gross weight; the product name says 1000gr, so the net content is 1 kg. Treating gross weight as net content overstates the pack by 25%.

“Karadeniz” is not a five-province blend

Turkey's Black Sea tea belt does include several provinces: Rize, Trabzon, Artvin, Giresun and Ordu. But “the belt contains these provinces” and “this product is a five-province blend” are different statements.

The official product page does not publish:

  • the share of each province in the blend;
  • the specific garden or factory;
  • the tea cultivar;
  • the harvest season;
  • the source or addition rate of the bergamot flavour.

This page therefore does not claim a five-province blend, and it does not assign a percentage to any province. What can be said is narrower: it is a black tea made from Turkish leaf with a bergamot flavour; the regional background is the Black Sea tea belt, not a five-origin formula.

The old draft's “2010 Black Sea corridor project,” “2013 launch” and “Karadeniz Koyu/Bahar versions” also lack official support. What the pack and page establish is the Bergamot Aromalı Karadeniz Çayı 1000gr SKU.

Bergamot is a flavour, not a cultivar

Bergamot is a citrus fruit; Earl Grey is the best-known bergamot-flavoured black tea. Çaykur's bergamot version belongs to that flavour family, but its base, grade and Turkish brewing context differ from an English Earl Grey blend.

Three layers need separating:

  1. Cultivar: the Camellia sinensis variety, which sets the tea's own aromatic base;
  2. Added flavour: bergamot oil, spice or natural flavour, which sets the citrus top note;
  3. Blend: the combination of origins, grades and seasons, which sets strength and cost.

“Bergamot Aromalı” describes layer 2 only. It says nothing about layer 1 or layer 3. Calling bergamot a “tea cultivar,” or saying the citrus note is “natural to the tea region,” is inaccurate.

Çaykur and the Rize tea region

Çaykur is Turkey's state-owned tea enterprise, established by Law No. 112 in 1983. It operates 47 wet-tea factories and one packaging factory, with a fresh-leaf capacity of about 6,600 tonnes a day. That makes it a central institution of the national tea system, not a small farm brand.

Rize is Turkey's core tea region, supplying about 67% of the country's fresh leaf. Of the 207 tea factories nationwide, 47 belong to Çaykur and 160 are private. This structure explains why Çaykur's range runs from premium bud tea to mass-market bulk packs: it serves a very large domestic market.

Turkish tea culture also shapes the product. Tea is normally brewed in a double teapot (çaydanlık): strong tea in the upper pot, hot water below, then diluted to taste in a tulip-shaped glass. A 1 kg bulk pack like Karadeniz is built for households, tea houses and small businesses, not for a one-off gift box.

Brewing: the double-teapot method

For the everyday Turkish method:

  1. put about one teaspoon of tea per glass into the upper pot;
  2. boil water in the lower pot and pour it over the tea;
  3. keep the upper pot warm for 15–20 minutes to build strength;
  4. pour the strong tea first, then top with hot water to taste;
  5. serve in a tulip-shaped glass, with sugar on the side.

The bergamot version needs no extra flavouring. For a single cup, use about 3 g to 150–200 ml of 95°C water and steep for 3–5 minutes. It extracts faster than an Orthodox leaf, so do not leave it sitting.

Milk is not the traditional Turkish way. Bergamot's citrus note also clashes easily with milk; if you want a milk tea, test a small amount first rather than treating it like an English breakfast blend.

Who it suits

This SKU suits someone who wants a Turkish everyday bergamot-flavoured black tea at a bulk-pack price. It is not a single-estate tea or a premium gift box; it is closer to a household staple.

If you want an unflavoured Turkish black tea, look at pure-tea lines such as Filiz, Altınbaş or Rize Turist. If you want an Earl Grey-style citrus tea but are used to an English blend, this product's base and brewing method are different. Decide whether you want “Turkish tea with bergamot” or “English Earl Grey,” and the purchase is less likely to go wrong.

Tea in Türkiye

Turkish tea is concentrated on the eastern Black Sea coast in Rize, Trabzon and Artvin. Black tea dominates, with Çaykur and private factories, the çaydanlık double pot and tulip glass shaping national tea culture.

United States

Abigail's Blend

Abigail's Blend is a commemorative black-tea blend created for the Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum and Abigail's Tea Room by tea master Bruce Richardson and crafted by Elmwood Inn Fine Teas. It is named for Abigail Adams. It is not the tea thrown into Boston Harbor in 1773, and it is not the “Boston Harbour Tea” commercial brand. The blend uses Chinese Yunnan black tea as its base and adds Assam, Nilgiri and Kenyan black teas. The museum's own description is that it recalls the Chinese black teas thrown overboard in 1773, “but better”. It can be served hot or iced, with or without milk. The product comes as 3.5 oz (99 g) loose tea or 14 pyramid tea bags at $17.99. On 16 December 1773, 340 chests of East India Company tea from the Beaver, Dartmouth and Eleanor were dumped into Boston Harbor, weighing over 92,000 lb (about 46 tonnes); about 80% was Chinese black tea.

United States

American Classic Tea (Charleston Tea Garden)

American Classic Tea is the flagship black tea of Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina. It is made exclusively from tea grown in the United States, but it is not a cultivar, a protected origin or a generic “Charleston-style” black tea. The garden's own material supplies the usable facts: 127 acres, more than 320 tea varieties, a history that runs from Charles Shepard's Pinehurst plantation in 1888 to the 1963 transplant at Wadmalaw Island, commercial operation in 1987 and Bigelow ownership from 2003. The tea itself is a larger-leaf black tea; the official brewing suggestion is 8 ounces of boiling water for 2–4 minutes, hot or iced. The page deliberately leaves cultivar and CTC/Orthodox claims out because the official pages do not publish them.

Assam Tea

Assam is the world’s largest contiguous tea-growing region, producing bold, malty black tea on the Brahmaputra plains; CTC is its everyday volume tea, while orthodox Assam offers a fuller-leaf alternative.
