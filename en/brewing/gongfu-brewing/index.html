Gongfu brewing

Brewing · 工夫泡法

A brewing style that uses a small pot or gaiwan, a relatively high leaf ratio and repeated short infusions. It is common in southern Fujian, Chaozhou and Taiwan, but it is not the only refined way to brew tea.

What it is

Gongfu brewing emphasises vessels, hand technique and repeated short infusions. It commonly uses a small pot or gaiwan, a relatively high leaf-to-water ratio and small tasting cups. The aim is to watch aroma and flavour change across infusions. It is not the only refined way to brew tea, nor does it require a complicated ritual; the core is controlling concentration and sharing the tea.

Basic sequence

  1. Warm the gaiwan or pot, the pitcher and the cups with hot water.
  2. Add leaf according to vessel size and tea type; gongfu brewing is usually stronger than a large mug infusion.
  3. Rinse if appropriate. Not every tea needs a rinse.
  4. Pour a short infusion and decant promptly to avoid over-steeping.
  5. Pour into a pitcher to mix the liquor, then divide among the cups.
  6. Re-infuse, adjusting time according to aroma, taste and colour; later infusions are usually longer.

Parameters

Leaf ratio, water temperature and infusion time are the three main variables. A high leaf ratio requires fast decanting. Coarse, compressed or aged tea can take hotter water and longer contact; fine green tea may not suit a high leaf ratio and long infusion. Do not rely on a fixed number of seconds. Taste the liquor and inspect the leaf, then adjust the next infusion.

Vessels

A gaiwan makes the leaf easy to observe and decant; a small pot holds heat better; a pitcher evens out the liquor; small cups suit tasting. More equipment is not automatically better. Choose vessels that are comfortable, appropriately sized and easy to clean.

Setting the table

Gongfu brewing is not about complexity but about reach: gaiwan or small pot in the brewing zone, pitcher to the right-front, cups in a row, waste bowl at the left hand. Keep the kettle an arm’s length away so you need not stand. Put the towel where drips are most likely. The set need not match; capacity matters more: 100–130 ml gaiwan, a slightly larger pitcher, 40–60 ml cups.

The pour rhythm

Pour fast for the first two infusions to wake the leaf, then lengthen from the third according to aroma and body. Do not leave the lid open between infusions or aroma escapes; do not let the leaf dry completely. For guests, collect in the pitcher before dividing so every cup is even. Keep the kettle hot rather than reheating each time.

More misconceptions

Gongfu is not “more leaf, more aroma”: excess leaf turns bitter and cuts endurance; nor is “hotter water, better tea”, which stews light oolong and green tea. Pour time is not a fixed number of seconds but follows tea, dose, temperature and vessel. Build your own baseline, then adjust by aroma and taste each infusion.

A repeatable baseline

For a 100 ml gaiwan and 5 g oolong: 95°C, quick rinse, 8 seconds first, 10 second, 15 seconds third, then add 5–10 seconds each. Green tea: 3 g, 80°C, 15 seconds first. Pu-erh: 7 g, 100°C, 10 seconds first. These are starting points; adjust each infusion by aroma, colour and taste.

Water, refills and pace

Water quality matters: hard water dulls aroma and roughens texture, while filtered or low-mineral water is steadier. Refill before the kettle is empty so level and temperature stay continuous; do not leave the gaiwan open between infusions. With guests, smell before dividing: aroma fades faster than taste.

More misconceptions

Gongfu is not a performance: graceful motion does not make good tea, and not every tea suits it—green tea is easier in a large pot. You need not time every infusion; with practice, aroma and colour guide you. Ware need not be expensive: handy, clean and the right size matters most.

Once practiced, gongfu brewing feels like a flexible household routine, not a fixed ceremony.

Common misconceptions

  • Is gongfu brewing the same as “kung fu tea”? The common Chinese term is gongfu cha, referring to time and skill, not martial arts.
  • Does more leaf always mean better tea? No. Too much leaf increases bitterness and waste.
  • Should every infusion use the same time? No. Adjust according to the tea and infusion number.
  • Do you need a complicated setup? No. A gaiwan and a pitcher are enough for a basic gongfu-style infusion.
